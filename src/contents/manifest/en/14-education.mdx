Schools and universities are where a society decides what kind of people it wants to raise. AI can explain a difficult idea at any hour, give feedback in seconds and relieve teachers of paperwork. It can also write the essay, solve the problem and let a student arrive at the end of the course without having learned anything. The first generation to grow up with these tools is a generation we are learning from. This chapter sets out how to make AI serve education rather than hollow it out.

## Principle

**14.1 Education forms people, not only outputs.** Its purpose is understanding, judgment, character and citizenship. AI serves learning. It does not replace it, and a good result is not the same as having learned.

**14.2 Effort is part of learning.** Struggle, practice, memory and the work of putting thoughts into words are how skills are built. AI should support that effort with hints, questions and feedback, and not remove it.

**14.3 Teachers are not replaceable.** The relationship between teacher and student is the heart of education. AI may help teachers plan, give feedback and handle administration, and the time saved goes back to students. Teachers keep authority over teaching and assessment.

**14.4 Every young person learns how AI works, and how to question it.** AI literacy, critical thinking and media literacy are part of basic education: what AI is, how it errs, where bias and privacy risks come from, how to verify, and how to use it responsibly. Teachers and parents need the same preparation.

**14.5 Children are protected.** Tools used by children are designed for their rights and wellbeing, with no profiling, no advertising, no manipulative engagement and no emotional exploitation. Younger children need stronger protections, and families and young people have a voice in how AI is used.

**14.6 Assessment must measure learning.** Exams and assignments are redesigned so that they show what a student knows and can do. Trust and fairness are built through clear rules and good design, and not through suspicion alone.

**14.7 Learners' data is for learners.** Data about students is kept to the minimum, protected, never sold and never used to label or limit a young person's future. Emotion recognition and intrusive surveillance have no place in education.

**14.8 An equal chance to learn with AI.** Devices, connectivity, teacher training and good tools reach every school and every language. Disadvantaged pupils must not be given only machines while others have teachers.

**14.9 Learning lasts a lifetime, and basic skills stay alive.** Adults have the right to retrain and to keep learning. Societies keep teaching reading, writing, calculation, reasoning and craft, since a skill that nobody practises disappears.

## Risks

- **The gap between performing and learning.** Students can produce better work with AI while learning less. When a chatbot does the reasoning, pupils skip the steps that build understanding, and their results fall once the tool is taken away.
- **Atrophy of core skills.** Writing, reasoning, memory, concentration and reading stamina fade when they are no longer practised.
- **Cheating and lost trust.** Assessments lose their meaning, and teachers and students are caught in an arms race.
- **Unreliable detection.** Tools that claim to detect AI writing can accuse innocent students, especially those writing in a second language, and punish them without proof.
- **Errors and bias in learning content.** Fluent but wrong explanations can teach false facts, and bias can shape what students absorb.
- **Surveillance and datafication.** Proctoring software, behaviour scoring and permanent profiles turn children into data, and student information is attractive to companies.
- **Companions and minors.** Chatbots that flatter, role-play intimacy or encourage dependence can harm children's development and wellbeing, and may expose them to inappropriate content.
- **Abuse with synthetic images.** Fake and humiliating images of classmates can be produced in seconds and cause serious harm.
- **Teacher deskilling and imposed tools.** Teachers may be pushed to adopt products they did not choose, or have their role reduced to supervising a system, and vendors can lock schools in.
- **Unequal education.** Wealthy families can combine human tutors with AI, while others have only the machine, and digital divides persist.
- **Homogenisation.** When everyone consults the same few systems, writing, ideas and viewpoints can converge.
- **Commercial capture.** Companies can shape curricula, offering free tools in exchange for data and access to children.
- **Unprepared adults.** Teachers and parents often have little training, and rules differ from class to class.
- **Loss of purpose.** If students ask "why learn what a machine can do?", the value of curiosity and mastery can be lost.

## Good practices

- **Decide the purpose of each task.** Ask what skill an exercise is meant to build. Use AI where it helps that learning, restrict it where it would replace it, and teach students to tell the difference.
- **Use tutors that teach.** Prefer tools designed for education that ask questions, offer hints, require the student's own attempt first and track learning and not only completion. Design and test them with teachers and evaluate them independently.
- **Keep practising without AI.** Include regular work done unaided: reading, writing, calculation, oral exchange and retrieval practice, so that skills stay alive.
- **Redesign assessment.** Use in-class writing, oral examinations, drafts and process portfolios, and projects in which students explain and defend their choices. State clearly when AI is allowed and how its use must be declared. Do not treat AI-detection scores as proof, and review suspected misconduct fairly, with a person and with the student heard.
- **Teach AI literacy.** Cover how models work in plain terms, their limits, bias, privacy, deepfakes, verification and ethics, with hands-on critical evaluation. Include teachers and parents, and give teachers paid time for training.
- **Respect teachers' agency.** Let teachers help choose tools, pilot them with evaluation, and opt out where appropriate. Consult their representatives and use the time saved for contact with students.
- **Protect children by design.** Use age-appropriate and safe-by-default settings, with no advertising or profiling and no emotional-companion features for young users. Have schools assess data protection before adopting a tool, set clear reporting channels for abuse, and act firmly against apps that create sexualised images. Respect rules requiring parental involvement for younger children.
- **Govern student data.** Collect the minimum, process it locally where possible, delete it at the end of a course, forbid its sale, limit vendors by contract and tell students and parents what is held.
- **Make access fair.** Provide devices, connectivity and training for under-resourced schools. Support public, open and multilingual educational AI, and build accessibility features such as reading aids, captions and translation with disabled students.
- **Build an evidence culture.** Evaluate tools before scaling, publish results, measure learning outcomes and not usage, and follow long-term effects on skills and wellbeing.
- **Keep the wider purpose.** Protect civic education, arts, sport, practical crafts and community life, and talk openly with students about why we learn.
- **Support lifelong learning.** Provide adult education, apprenticeships and genuine retraining, with libraries and community centres as places of learning.
- **Involve families.** Talk about AI use at home, agree on family rules, learn together, check privacy settings and watch for signs of unhealthy dependence.

## Concrete example

*Two hypothetical cases.*

**A secondary school.** A school introduces AI across the curriculum.

In the **wrong version**, every student gets a general chatbot with no guidance. Homework is completed by AI and grades rise, while exam results fall. Teachers use detection software that wrongly accuses a student who writes in a second language. The platform records pupils' behaviour and sells insights. The poorest students have no device at home, and a younger pupil forms a close attachment to a companion chatbot.

In the **right version**, the school sets out for each subject what AI may and may not do. It uses an educational tutor that asks questions and gives hints, includes in-class and oral assessment, and teaches AI literacy. Detection scores are never treated as proof. Student data is minimal and bound by contract, devices are lent to those who need them, teachers are trained and consulted, and after a year the school evaluates what students have actually learned.

**An essay.** A student has to write about a historical event.

In the **wrong version**, they ask an AI for the essay and hand it in.

In the **right version**, they write a draft, ask the AI to point out weak arguments and missing evidence, revise it, and then explain their choices to the teacher in a short conversation.

## What must not be done

- Replacing teachers with AI to cut costs, or treating AI tutoring as a substitute for human relationships.
- Letting AI do the thinking that an exercise is meant to teach, or rewarding output over understanding.
- Punishing students on the basis of AI-detection tools alone.
- Profiling, scoring or tracking children across their school life, selling or monetising their data, or using emotion recognition and invasive monitoring in schools.
- Exposing minors to companion chatbots designed to foster emotional dependence, to sexualised content, advertising or manipulative engagement.
- Using AI to create or spread sexualised or humiliating images of classmates or teachers.
- Imposing tools on teachers without consultation or training, or locking schools into a vendor.
- Creating a two-tier system in which disadvantaged students receive machines and others receive teachers.
- Presenting AI output to students as authoritative without checking it.
- Dropping foundational skills from the curriculum on the assumption that AI will supply them.
- Hiding the use of AI from students and parents, or leaving the rules unclear.
- Allowing marketing in classrooms or commercial control of what is taught.

## How to remedy

When AI has harmed learning, trust or children's wellbeing:

1. **Pause and review.** Suspend the harmful tool or policy, and talk with students, teachers and parents. Protect those affected.
2. **Repair learning.** Identify the gaps, offer targeted catch-up, rebuild skills with unaided practice, and withdraw sanctions based on unreliable detection, clearing students' records.
3. **Protect the data.** Audit what was collected, delete it, notify parents and the authority if there was a breach, and change vendor if necessary.
4. **Support teachers.** Give them training, time and a voice, and reverse tools that were imposed.
5. **Redesign tasks and assessment** so that they show real learning.
6. **Evaluate and publish.** Commission an independent evaluation, share the findings and require evidence before scaling up.
7. **Care for the children involved.** Offer counselling and guidance to families in cases of dependence. Where synthetic images were used to harass, protect the victim, apply school and legal procedures to those responsible and report to the authorities.
8. **Update the rules** and strengthen AI literacy for everyone.

**If you are a student wrongly accused of using AI,** ask to see the evidence and request a review by a person. Keep your drafts and version history, and speak to your teacher, a trusted adult or the person responsible for student complaints.

The aim of education is not to produce answers. It is to form people who can ask good questions, judge the answers and take responsibility for what they do with them.
