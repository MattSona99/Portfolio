War is the field in which the cost of a mistake is paid in human lives, and in which the pressure to adopt every new technology is strongest. AI is entering it at every level: from weapons that select targets, to software that recommends them, to attacks on networks and campaigns of deception that never fire a shot. Nations have a right to defend themselves. The question this chapter answers is how, and where the limits lie.

## Principle

**3.1 No machine decides to kill.** The decision to use lethal force against a human being is taken by a human being, who understands the situation, can be held to account, and has the real ability to stop. This is the fourth pillar of this manifesto, and the one that admits no exception.

**3.2 The law of war applies in full.** The rules of humanitarian law, namely distinction between combatants and civilians, proportionality, precaution and humanity, apply to AI-enabled weapons and tools exactly as they apply to any other. A system that cannot respect them must not be used.

**3.3 Speed must not erase judgment.** AI that recommends targets or prepares attack decisions must never compress human deliberation to the point where approval becomes a reflex. A faster war is not a better one.

**3.4 The gravest decisions stay entirely human.** AI must never be given authority over the use of nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction. Decisions of that magnitude belong to accountable people, with time to think and to doubt.

**3.5 Civilians and their infrastructure are protected everywhere.** Hospitals, water, power, food supply, communications, financial systems and electoral processes are not targets, in the physical world or in cyberspace. An attack made by software is an attack.

**3.6 Hybrid war is war, with no loophole.** Disinformation campaigns, impersonation, deepfakes and sabotage carried out below the threshold of open conflict are not made acceptable by deniability or by the absence of bloodshed. Their purpose is to harm populations, and they are judged by that.

**3.7 People are not profiled for persecution.** AI must not be used to identify, track or target people because of their ethnicity, religion, opinions or community, nor to build apparatuses of mass surveillance and control over a population.

**3.8 Responsibility extends along the whole chain.** Commanders, governments, developers and suppliers each answer for what AI does in war. Claims that autonomy makes war more precise must be shown by independent evidence, not assumed.

**3.9 Peace is the purpose.** Defence exists to protect people. AI should also serve peace: early warning, verification of agreements, demining, humanitarian logistics and the protection of civilians.

## Risks

- **Arms race and machine-speed escalation.** When systems react to each other faster than humans can understand, a crisis can escalate before diplomacy has a chance to act.
- **The accountability gap.** When a machine selects a target, it becomes tempting to say that no one decided, and that no one is responsible.
- **A lower threshold for war.** If using force costs fewer of one's own soldiers, it becomes easier to decide to use it.
- **Errors repeated at scale.** A flaw in data, a bias or a vulnerability to deception does not cause one mistake but thousands, identical and simultaneous.
- **Misidentification.** Systems trained in one context behave unpredictably in another, and classifying a person from patterns of data is never certain.
- **Proliferation.** As the technology becomes cheaper, it reaches non-state armed groups, criminal networks and regimes with no interest in the rules.
- **Attacks on the civilian fabric.** AI can make it easier to find weaknesses and to automate attacks on networks on which hospitals, cities and economies depend, with effects that spread beyond any intended target.
- **Industrial-scale deception.** Convincing falsehoods, produced cheaply and in volume, can inflame conflict, impersonate leaders and wear down the public's trust in anything it sees or hears.
- **Moral distance.** The further the person is from the consequence, the easier it is to forget that someone is on the other side.

## Good practices

- **Make meaningful human control a design requirement.** Set limits on the duration, area, type of target and conditions of use, and always keep the ability to abort.
- **Review new means of warfare before use.** Subject every new weapon, and every AI decision-support system, to a legal review before development, acquisition and deployment, as humanitarian law already requires for new weapons.
- **Test under realistic and hostile conditions.** Include deception, unfamiliar environments and degraded communications. If a system's behaviour cannot be predicted, it is not deployed.
- **Protect decision-makers' time and information.** Show sources and uncertainty, give people enough time to question the recommendation, and record both the machine's suggestion and the human's decision.
- **Measure and investigate civilian harm.** Track it systematically, investigate it independently, and report publicly whenever possible.
- **Keep nuclear command in human hands.** Maintain an explicit rule that no AI system has authority over such decisions, and support it with international commitments.
- **Defend the civilian infrastructure.** Build resilience, keep manual fallbacks, share vulnerabilities responsibly, and agree on norms that put hospitals, water, power and elections out of bounds.
- **Counter hybrid threats with transparency.** Authenticate official communications, mark synthetic media, support independent fact-checking, and invest in the public's media literacy.
- **Control the spread.** Apply export controls and customer checks to military-grade and dual-use AI, and protect models and tools from theft.
- **Protect the right to refuse.** Engineers, analysts and soldiers must be able to decline to take part in unlawful uses, and to report them without retaliation.
- **Support binding international rules.** Contribute to the negotiation of enforceable limits on autonomous weapons, with verification and transparency.

## Concrete example

*A hypothetical case.* An armed force introduces AI decision-support to propose targets from aerial imagery and intercepted communications.

In the **wrong version**, the system produces hundreds of suggestions a day. Officers have seconds to approve each, and the system does not show how it reached its conclusions. Patterns in the data cause civilians to be classified as combatants. After a strike on a civilian building, nobody can say why it was chosen, and nobody is held accountable.

In the **right version**, the system only flags areas for review. Analysts see the sources and the uncertainty, and have enough time and authority to reject a suggestion. A legal adviser reviews every high-risk case. Hard limits define what may never be struck. Each decision is recorded together with the system's recommendation, civilian harm is assessed after every operation by someone independent of the unit, and the lessons feed back into training, rules and the design of the system.

## What must not be done

- Using weapons that select and attack people without meaningful human control, above all systems designed to target human beings on the basis of sensor data or profiles.
- Giving AI any authority over nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction.
- Using AI to attack hospitals, water and power systems, food supply, communications, financial systems or electoral processes.
- Running deceptive campaigns that impersonate real people or institutions, or that fabricate evidence, to incite violence or to deceive a population.
- Using AI to identify and persecute people by ethnicity, religion or belief, or to build mass surveillance for repression.
- Hiding behind the machine: blaming "the system" for a decision that people chose to delegate.
- Developing systems whose behaviour cannot be tested, predicted or explained to those who must answer for them.
- Building weapons designed to resist human intervention, or software that spreads and attacks on its own beyond anyone's control.
- Supplying military-grade AI to actors who are likely to use it against civilians, or leaving it unprotected where it can be stolen.

## How to remedy

When AI has contributed to unlawful harm or to a loss of control in a military context:

1. **Stop and protect.** Suspend the system or the operation, prevent further harm and protect the civilians affected.
2. **Investigate independently and promptly.** Preserve records, logs and recommendations, and establish what went wrong, including the role of the machine, the data and the people.
3. **Acknowledge and repair.** Recognise the harm publicly, assist the victims and provide compensation or other forms of reparation.
4. **Hold people to account at every level.** Commanders, officials, developers and suppliers answer for their part. "The algorithm selected it" is not a defence.
5. **Fix the system and the rules.** Withdraw or redesign the system, revise the conditions of use, training and legal reviews.
6. **For attacks on infrastructure or information.** Restore essential services first, attribute responsibility carefully and on evidence, publish countermeasures, and rebuild public trust through transparency.
7. **Share the lessons.** Report to international bodies and take part in building shared rules, so that a failure in one place becomes a safeguard everywhere.

A war can only be fought within limits if someone is still answerable for each act. AI must never make that impossible.
