The earlier chapters speak of people, and for good reason. But the living world is not a backdrop to human affairs. Every datacentre stands on land that was once a habitat, every chip contains minerals taken from an ecosystem, and every algorithm that optimises fishing, farming or logging acts on beings who cannot object. At the same time, AI can help us count species, catch poachers and restore forests. Discussions of AI and the environment mostly concern carbon and water. This chapter speaks directly of plants, animals and ecosystems.

## Principle

**18.1 Nature has value in itself.** Forests, rivers, oceans, species and ecosystems are not only resources. They have worth beyond their usefulness to us, and human life depends on them. The ethics of AI must include the living world, not only humans.

**18.2 Animals are sentient, and their welfare counts.** Scientific opinion increasingly holds that many animals, including vertebrates and some invertebrates, may be capable of experience. Where there is a realistic possibility, their welfare is taken into account. AI is not used to increase animal suffering for the sake of efficiency or profit.

**18.3 The footprint on habitats is counted.** Data centres, mines, power lines, quarries, water extraction, light and noise affect habitats, migration and breeding. The mitigation hierarchy applies: avoid harm first, then minimise it, then restore, and use offsets only as a last resort.

**18.4 Use AI to protect nature, and prove that it works.** Monitoring, detection, restoration and modelling are worthwhile when they lead to action in the field, and they are judged by results for species and ecosystems, not by the quality of a dashboard.

**18.5 No digital shortcut for field knowledge.** Models and maps can miss what rangers, local communities and indigenous peoples know. Conservation priorities must not be set by gaps and biases in data.

**18.6 Wildlife data is sensitive.** The location of rare species, nests and migration routes can serve poachers and traffickers. Openness is balanced with protection, and monitoring must not itself disturb or harm animals or people.

**18.7 The exploitation of nature is not automated.** AI-optimised fishing, logging, mining, intensive farming and livestock must not push extraction beyond ecological limits. Efficiency gains must not become a licence to take more.

**18.8 Listening to other species requires care.** AI that decodes animal communication, or that interacts with animals, may help their welfare and their protection. It could equally be used to lure, stress, hunt or exploit them. Such work puts the animals' interests first.

**18.9 Engineered life is not released lightly.** Modified organisms, gene drives and synthetic biology released into ecosystems cannot be recalled. AI-accelerated design raises the stakes, and calls for precaution, independent ecological assessment and the consent of the communities concerned.

**18.10 The benefits of nature are shared, and nature has a seat.** Genetic resources, sequence data and traditional knowledge used by AI lead to fair benefit-sharing with their countries and communities of origin. Ecological expertise takes part in AI governance, where nature is too often absent.

## Risks

- **Habitat loss and fragmentation.** Data centres, mines for chip minerals, grid lines and reservoirs convert and cut up habitats, and bring heat, noise, light and local pollution.
- **Pressure on rivers and wetlands.** Water withdrawal and warm discharge can harm aquatic life and the ecosystems that depend on them.
- **Intensified extraction.** Better prediction and automation can make logging, fishing, mining and farming more efficient at taking more than ecosystems can replace.
- **Animals as data points.** Precision livestock systems can raise throughput and density while hiding suffering behind metrics.
- **Poaching and trafficking.** Leaked location data, automated online trade and forged documents can serve wildlife crime.
- **Biased and patchy data.** Most open biodiversity records come from North America and Europe. Models can perform poorly elsewhere, overlook insects, fungi and soil life, and misdirect conservation funds.
- **Maps that replace the ground.** Remote models can overrule people who know the land, and conservation that ignores local communities can dispossess them.
- **Surveillance and disturbance.** Camera traps can record people without consent, and drones, collars and playback can stress or alter the behaviour of animals.
- **Technological distraction and greenwashing.** The promise of future AI solutions can delay protection that is available now, and claims of nature benefits can mask real harm.
- **Ecological accidents.** Engineered organisms or gene drives released into the wild could trigger cascading effects, and AI may make their design easier.
- **Biopiracy.** Genetic sequence data and traditional knowledge can be taken for use in AI without consent or benefit-sharing.
- **A governance blind spot.** Reports on AI's environmental impact tend to count carbon and water, and nature is hardly mentioned in global AI governance.
- **Human-only ethics.** Frameworks that count only human interests treat animals and ecosystems as externalities.

## Good practices

- **Assess impacts on nature.** Carry out biodiversity assessments for data centres, mines and grid projects. Keep out of protected areas, critical habitats, wetlands and migration corridors, choose sites with ecologists, monitor afterwards, restore damage, and treat offsets as real, additional and long-lasting, and as a last resort. Include nature-related risks in AI footprint reporting.
- **Reduce disturbance.** Control light and noise, use wildlife-friendly lighting and bird-safe design, keep corridors open, avoid sensitive seasons for construction and keep diesel backup away from vulnerable sites.
- **Protect aquatic ecosystems.** Keep environmental flows, protect wetlands and limit the temperature of discharged water.
- **Make conservation AI accountable to results.** Define the decision it supports, deploy it where it leads to action by rangers, enforcement or restoration, measure effects on species and patrols, fund maintenance, publish results and favour open tools and low-power devices.
- **Close the data gaps.** Invest in data collection in under-represented regions and groups of species, support community science, audit species coverage and require human verification for decisions about rare species.
- **Work with local and indigenous knowledge.** Co-design monitoring with communities, obtain their free, prior and informed consent, respect their data sovereignty, share benefits and create jobs for local rangers.
- **Protect sensitive wildlife data.** Blur or delay coordinates for threatened species, use tiered access and vetting, keep secure logs, remove geotags and keep rare-species locations out of public training data. Protect the safety of rangers.
- **Set welfare standards for animal technology.** Require independent welfare assessment of AI that monitors or manages animals on farms, in laboratories and in zoos, use AI to detect pain and disease early and to improve conditions and not only throughput, limit stocking density, and avoid welfare-washing. Where validated, use computational and other non-animal methods to reduce animal testing.
- **Hold extraction within limits.** Set catch, harvest and extraction caps on scientific advice and enforce them whatever the efficiency of the tools. Use satellites and AI to detect illegal fishing and deforestation, make alerts public, and require disclosure of AI use in extractive industries.
- **Review interspecies technology ethically.** Treat it like animal research: independent ethical review, non-invasive methods, no playback that distresses or manipulates, no luring, hunting or commercial exploitation, open protocols and benefits to local communities.
- **Secure ecological safety in biotechnology.** Require independent ecological risk assessment, containment, staged field trials with local consent and plans for reversal, international coordination, and screening of AI-assisted designs for ecological risk.
- **Share benefits.** Provide fair compensation and credit for the use of genetic sequence data and traditional knowledge, and have AI companies contribute to biodiversity finance in proportion to their footprint.
- **Put nature in governance.** Seat ecologists, conservation bodies and indigenous representatives in AI governance and standard-setting, and make nature metrics part of AI impact reports and scientific assessments.
- **As an individual or organisation,** choose lighter models, avoid publishing sensitive wildlife locations on citizen-science apps, report wildlife crime, reduce light and noise at night, and protect the nature around you.
- **Teach ecological literacy** together with AI literacy.

## Concrete example

*Two hypothetical cases.*

**A camera-trap network.** A conservation group deploys AI to identify animals from camera-trap images in a reserve.

In the **wrong version**, a public dashboard shows the exact location of threatened animals. The model, trained mostly on species from other continents, misidentifies local animals. The local community was not consulted and is barred from land it has used for generations. When funding stops, the devices are abandoned.

In the **right version**, locations are blurred and access is tiered. The model is tuned with local data and checked by rangers. The community co-designs the project, benefits from jobs and keeps its rights, results for species are measured and published, and maintenance is funded.

**An AI-optimised fishing fleet.** A company uses AI to find fish more efficiently.

In the **wrong version**, catches exceed sustainable limits, bycatch rises and the stock declines.

In the **right version**, the same tools operate under strict, science-based quotas with public monitoring, and are used to cut bycatch and to enforce closed areas.

## What must not be done

- Siting AI infrastructure or mines in protected areas, critical habitats, wetlands or migration corridors, or ignoring the mitigation hierarchy.
- Using AI to scale up logging, fishing, mining or farming beyond science-based ecological limits.
- Using AI to raise animal throughput at the expense of welfare, hiding suffering behind dashboards or treating animals as mere data points.
- Publishing or leaking the location of threatened species or sensitive sites in ways that can enable poaching or trafficking, or using AI to automate illegal wildlife trade.
- Using claims of nature benefits, biodiversity credits or offsets to greenwash a harmful project.
- Replacing local and indigenous knowledge with remote models, imposing conservation that dispossesses communities, or monitoring local people without their consent.
- Using decoded animal signals to lure, hunt, stress or exploit animals, or deploying robots or playback that harm or disturb wildlife.
- Releasing engineered organisms or gene drives into the wild without independent ecological assessment and the consent of local communities.
- Taking genetic sequence data or traditional knowledge for use in AI without fair benefit-sharing.
- Leaving nature out of AI impact assessments, standards and governance, or reporting only carbon and water.
- Using the promise of future AI benefits to postpone protection that is available now.

## How to remedy

When AI infrastructure or practices have harmed nature or animals:

1. **Stop the harm and protect wildlife.** Halt the damaging work, protect nests and breeding seasons, take emergency measures for affected species and arrange rescue where needed.
2. **Assess the ecological damage.** Commission independent ecologists to compare the baseline with the present state, identify the species and processes affected, and publish the findings.
3. **Restore.** Rewet and replant with native species, remove barriers, compensate for lost ecosystem services and monitor for the long term. Use offsets only for what cannot be avoided or restored.
4. **Make amends to communities.** Compensate people, restore access and rights, stop surveillance without consent, return data and adopt co-management.
5. **Secure sensitive data.** If wildlife data has leaked, remove it, restrict access, alert rangers and authorities, increase patrols and pursue those responsible.
6. **Fix the system.** Relocate or redesign the project, add safeguards, update impact assessments, retrain models for local species, correct welfare failures and withdraw harmful tools.
7. **Enforce accountability.** Apply penalties, licence conditions and liability for ecological damage, require reporting and protect whistleblowers.
8. **Share the lessons.** Write nature into standards and into the next assessment.

We share the planet with other beings who cannot sign declarations or lodge complaints. A technology that claims to be intelligent should show it in how it treats those who can ask it for nothing.
