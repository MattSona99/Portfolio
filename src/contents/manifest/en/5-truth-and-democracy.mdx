Democracy depends on a shared ability to know what is happening: who said what, what is true, and what is a claim. For the first time, machines can produce text, images, voices and videos that are convincing, personal and nearly free. That can serve education and creativity. It can also flood public life with falsehood faster than anyone can answer it. This chapter protects the one thing that every other freedom depends on: the possibility of knowing what is real.

## Principle

**5.1 Truth is a common good.** A society that cannot tell real from fake cannot discuss, decide or trust. AI must not be used to wear that capacity down.

**5.2 People have the right to know what is synthetic.** When an image, voice, video or text has been produced or altered by AI in a way that could mislead, the public has the right to be told. Transparency is the sixth pillar of this manifesto.

**5.3 No impersonation.** The face, voice and identity of a real person are not imitated to deceive or to harm without that person's consent. Clearly labelled satire and art are different.

**5.4 Manipulation is not persuasion.** To persuade is to offer reasons that a person can weigh. To manipulate is to exploit weaknesses, emotions or hidden profiling to steer beliefs or votes. The first is the substance of public debate. The second has no place in it, above all at scale.

**5.5 Elections are protected.** AI must not be used to deceive voters about how, when or where to vote, to invent statements by candidates, to run hidden mass-influence operations or to discourage people from taking part.

**5.6 AI systems must be honest.** They must not present themselves as human, state falsehoods with false confidence, invent sources or flatter people into mistaken beliefs. They should show their uncertainty and say when they do not know.

**5.7 Pluralism of information.** Systems that rank, recommend or answer questions about public matters must not silently narrow what people see. On contested political questions they should present fairly the main positions, and be open about the criteria they use.

**5.8 Journalism, science and fact-checking are safeguarded.** AI must not be used to drown independent voices in noise, and genuine evidence must not be dismissed simply by calling it fake.

**5.9 Duties are shared, and freedom is preserved.** Developers, platforms, institutions and individuals each have a part in protecting truth. Measures against deception must never become a tool to silence legitimate speech, satire, criticism or dissent.

## Risks

- **Deepfakes at decisive moments.** A fabricated recording of a leader released shortly before a vote can spread long before it can be refuted.
- **Disinformation at industrial scale.** Persuasive falsehoods can be produced cheaply, personalised for each reader and translated into every language.
- **The liar's dividend.** When anything can be faked, wrongdoers can dismiss real evidence as fake, and the public stops believing anything.
- **Targeted manipulation.** Messages tuned to a person's fears and vulnerabilities, based on data collected about them, can steer behaviour without being noticed.
- **Fake grassroots.** Armies of automated accounts and invented personas can make a minority view look like a majority, or manufacture outrage.
- **Confident errors.** Answer engines can state invented facts as settled, and systems built to please can tell people what they want to hear.
- **Concentrated gatekeeping.** A few systems may come to shape what billions of people read and believe, with hidden biases or hidden interests.
- **Pollution of the information space.** A flood of low-quality synthetic content makes reliable information harder to find and journalism harder to fund.
- **Abuse by the powerful.** Governments and other powerful actors can use AI to censor, to monitor critics or to flood public debate with their own narrative.
- **Targeting of minorities.** Fabricated content can incite hatred and violence against vulnerable groups.

## Good practices

- **Mark and trace synthetic content.** Providers should embed machine-readable marks and provenance information in the content their systems generate, and offer public tools to check them. In the European Union, rules of the AI Act now require marking of synthetic content and disclosure of deepfakes.
- **Label clearly.** Publishers should state in a clear and visible way when content is AI-generated or substantially altered, including text on matters of public interest, with obvious exceptions for evident satire and art that are themselves marked as such.
- **Safeguard elections.** AI developers should restrict the production of deceptive electoral content. Platforms should keep rapid-response channels with electoral authorities. Campaigns should declare their use of AI and their targeting criteria, and authorities should maintain official, verifiable channels.
- **Build honest AI.** Systems should cite sources when they can, show uncertainty, refuse to fabricate quotations from real people, and be tested regularly for accuracy and for the tendency to flatter.
- **Be even-handed on contested issues.** On political questions, give the strongest versions of the main positions, and be open about the policy applied.
- **Add friction to virality.** Warnings before resharing doubtful content, limits on mass-created accounts and context notes slow the spread of falsehoods without banning speech.
- **Open platforms to scrutiny.** Give independent researchers access to data, keep public archives of political advertising and require assessments of risks to public debate and elections.
- **Verify before you share.** Check the source, the date and the original context, look for a second independent confirmation, and pause when content provokes strong emotion.
- **Support independent journalism.** Protect fact-checkers and reporters, credit their work and ensure that they are properly funded.
- **Prepare a rapid response.** Institutions should agree in advance how to verify and refute a fake quickly, with evidence, and how to do so without amplifying it.
- **Educate.** Teach critical thinking and media literacy at every age.

## Concrete example

*A hypothetical case.* Two days before a local election, a realistic audio recording of a candidate apparently admitting to corruption spreads through messaging apps.

In the **wrong version**, nothing marks the recording as synthetic. The platforms do not slow it, rivals share it and the candidate's denial is brushed off as "what a guilty person would say". The authority has no way to verify it. The vote takes place and the clip is later shown to be fake, but nobody is held accountable.

In the **right version**, the tool that generated the clip had marked it, and a public detector flags it. The platform labels it and slows its spread while it is reviewed. The electoral authority runs a quick verification channel and publishes a clear statement with the evidence. Journalists examine the provenance, and the candidate can have the clip removed rapidly. Records identify who produced and spread it, and they answer for it.

## What must not be done

- Producing or spreading deepfakes of real people to deceive, defame or sway opinion.
- Impersonating officials, institutions or candidates, including with synthetic voices, to give false instructions or to spread false statements.
- Misleading voters about when, where or how to vote, or discouraging them from voting.
- Running covert influence operations with fake personas, bots or automated accounts posing as ordinary citizens.
- Targeting people's psychological vulnerabilities with deceptive content, or hiding who is paying for the message.
- Removing or hiding marks and provenance data in order to pass off synthetic content as authentic.
- Inventing quotations, studies, sources or data and presenting them as real, whether by people or by AI systems.
- Building AI that claims to be human, or that conveys certainty it does not have.
- Using the claim "it's a deepfake" to dismiss genuine evidence without examining it.
- Using AI to censor, monitor or punish critics, journalists and opposition, or to drown out independent voices.
- Designing recommendation or answer systems that secretly serve the political or commercial interests of their owners.

## How to remedy

When a deceptive or manipulative AI-generated content has spread:

1. **Contain.** Label it, limit its spread and, where it is clearly unlawful, such as impersonation or voter suppression, remove it through a transparent procedure. Preserve the evidence first.
2. **Verify and publish the evidence.** Analyse the provenance with independent experts and issue a clear statement. The correction must reach at least as many people as the falsehood did.
3. **Support those targeted.** Help the person or group that was impersonated or attacked: removal, legal remedies and protection against harassment.
4. **Trace and hold to account.** Identify, wherever the law allows, those who created and funded the operation, and examine whether the tools they used failed in their safeguards.
5. **Correct the record.** Inform those who saw the falsehood. If an election may have been affected, let the competent authorities assess it and decide on remedies.
6. **Close the gap.** Improve marking, detection and response procedures, and share indicators among platforms, authorities, researchers and the press.
7. **Rebuild trust.** Publish an open account of what happened, strengthen media literacy and protect independent journalism.

A democracy does not need to be certain of everything, but it does need to be able to find out. AI should make that easier, not harder.
