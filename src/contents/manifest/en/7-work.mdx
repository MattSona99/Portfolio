Work is how most people earn a living, build skills, find a place in society and give shape to their days. AI will remove some jobs, transform many others and create new ones. Estimates of how many differ widely, but almost all agree on two points: the change will be large, and the new jobs will not automatically go to the people who lose the old ones. This chapter is about making sure that the transition is fair, and that people are treated as people while it happens.

## Principle

**7.1 Work is more than income.** It is also dignity, identity, belonging and a way of contributing. A society that automates work must also ask what people will do, and who will benefit.

**7.2 Gains and costs are shared.** The value created by AI-driven productivity must reach the people who bear the cost of the change, not only those who own the systems. Employers, governments and workers each have a part in a fair transition.

**7.3 Honesty about change.** Employers and governments must say early and plainly what is planned. AI must not be used as a pretext for dismissals that have other reasons, nor its effects hidden from those who will feel them.

**7.4 A real right to retrain.** Workers whose tasks are automated are entitled to paid time, real training and support towards work that exists, not to an empty promise of "upskilling".

**7.5 People decide about people.** Hiring, firing, promotion, discipline, pay and evaluation cannot be left to an algorithm alone. A person must be able to understand the decision, contest it and speak to a human being.

**7.6 No exhausting surveillance.** Monitoring workers with AI must be proportionate, open and agreed. People are not to be scored continuously, read for emotions or driven at a pace that damages their health.

**7.7 Invisible work is work.** The people who label data, moderate content and test systems deserve fair pay, contracts, safe conditions and psychological support, wherever in the world they are.

**7.8 Workers have a voice.** Workers and their representatives are consulted before AI is introduced into their work, and take part in shaping how it is used.

**7.9 The next generation needs a way in.** If entry-level tasks disappear, new ways to learn a trade or a profession must take their place, so that young people can still gain experience and move on.

## Risks

- **Concentrated displacement.** Some occupations, such as administrative work, customer service, translation, certain creative and junior professional tasks, may change faster than people can retrain, and some regions may be hit harder than others.
- **Aggregate gains, individual losses.** A net increase in jobs on paper does not help a person whose own job has gone, if the new jobs require other skills, are in other places or pay less.
- **Polarisation.** Gains may flow to highly skilled workers and to owners of capital, while middle-income jobs thin out and wages come under pressure.
- **A broken first rung.** When AI takes over the tasks through which juniors used to learn, the pipeline of future experts runs dry.
- **AI as an excuse.** Layoffs presented as technological necessity can conceal ordinary cost-cutting, and the threat of automation can be used to depress wages.
- **Algorithmic management.** Opaque systems assign shifts, set the pace, rate performance and deactivate accounts without explanation or appeal.
- **Surveillance and stress.** Constant monitoring, productivity scoring and emotion analysis erode privacy, trust and health.
- **Hidden labour.** Annotators and moderators, often in poorer countries, may be underpaid and exposed to harmful content without support.
- **Biased hiring tools.** Systems trained on past decisions can repeat past discrimination, at scale and out of sight.
- **Global imbalance.** Countries differ both in exposure and in their ability to adapt, and economies built on outsourced services may be especially vulnerable.
- **Loss of skill and meaning.** If AI does the thinking and people only supervise, craft and judgment can wither.
- **Ignored work.** Care work, informal work and unpaid work are poorly captured by the measures used to judge the transition.

## Good practices

- **Give early notice and consult.** Assess the impact of AI on jobs before large deployments, share the findings with workers and their representatives, and plan the transition with them.
- **Redeploy before dismissing.** Offer retraining and reassignment first, and treat dismissal as the last resort.
- **Fund real retraining.** Provide paid time for training, recognised qualifications, guidance and portable training entitlements, funded by employers and by public programmes.
- **Keep humans in decisions about people.** Audit hiring and evaluation tools for bias, tell candidates and employees when they are used and guarantee human review and appeal.
- **Set limits on monitoring.** Allow it only for stated and proportionate purposes, agree it with workers' representatives, prohibit covert surveillance and emotion recognition, give people access to their own data and forbid automatic dismissal or deactivation without human review.
- **Treat data workers fairly.** Pay a living wage, provide written contracts, limit exposure to harmful content, offer psychological support, and hold companies responsible for the conditions of their suppliers and subcontractors.
- **Negotiate AI at work.** Include AI in collective bargaining and joint committees, and test new systems with the people who will use them.
- **Share the gains.** Consider productivity-linked pay, shorter working time where feasible, profit-sharing and fair taxation of the gains from automation. Strengthen unemployment protection and give income support during retraining, with regional funds for the places most affected.
- **Protect the entry-level path.** Keep apprenticeships and junior roles, and design them so that AI helps people to learn rather than replaces their learning.
- **Design for augmentation.** Organise tasks so that workers keep their skills and judgment, and measure the quality of work and not only the quantity produced.
- **Support small businesses and public services.** Help them to adopt AI in ways that serve their workers and customers.
- **Think internationally.** Support decent-work standards for platform and data work, and help poorer countries to build skills and to take part fairly in the AI economy.
- **Measure and publish.** Track and report jobs lost, created and redeployed, and changes in wages, so that policy follows facts and not fear or hype.

## Concrete example

*A hypothetical case.* A company with a customer-service centre of 300 people introduces an AI assistant.

In the **wrong version**, management announces dismissals overnight and attributes them to AI, although much of the motive is cost-cutting. The remaining agents are monitored by real-time speed and sentiment scores. The system is trained on their recorded calls without their agreement, data labelling is outsourced to a contractor that pays very little, and those who are dismissed receive a week's notice and a link to an online course.

In the **right version**, the company consults employees and their representatives a year ahead and publishes an assessment. The AI handles routine requests, and agents move to complex cases, quality control and the supervision and improvement of the system. They receive paid training time and recognised certificates. Those whose roles do disappear are offered redeployment or a supported transition with income protection. Monitoring is limited to improving quality and agreed with representatives. The labelling partner meets fair-pay standards. Part of the savings is shared through shorter hours or bonuses, part is invested in service quality, and junior staff still have a path in.

## What must not be done

- Using AI as a pretext to dismiss people, or concealing the real reasons for layoffs.
- Dismissing large numbers of people without notice, consultation or realistic alternatives.
- Letting algorithms alone hire, fire, discipline, rank, pay or deactivate workers.
- Monitoring workers in secret, using emotion recognition, or scoring them relentlessly at a pace that harms them.
- Using monitoring data for purposes that workers were never told about.
- Training AI on workers' output, voice or likeness without their consent and fair terms.
- Outsourcing data labelling and moderation to precarious, underpaid and unsupported workers.
- Keeping every gain for the owners of the systems while the costs fall on workers and the public.
- Removing entry-level paths without putting anything in their place.
- Using hiring tools that have not been tested for bias, or hiding their use from candidates.
- Offering "retraining" in name only, with no time, money or real jobs at the end.
- Requiring people to train the system that will replace them without any guarantee for their future.

## How to remedy

When AI has been introduced in ways that harm workers:

1. **Pause and talk.** Suspend contested deployments or dismissals and open real dialogue with workers and their representatives.
2. **Offer concrete support.** Provide income continuity, paid retraining, redeployment, help to find new work and continuity of health cover, with priority for the most vulnerable.
3. **Correct unfair decisions.** Review automated decisions about hiring, discipline and pay. Reinstate and compensate where decisions were wrong, and withdraw biased tools.
4. **End abusive monitoring.** Stop it, delete data that was collected unlawfully and give workers access to what was collected about them.
5. **Improve the conditions of data workers.** Raise pay, provide contracts, handle harmful content safely, offer mental-health support and audit suppliers.
6. **Share the gains.** Renegotiate how the benefits of productivity are distributed, and invest in sectors and regions that were hit.
7. **Measure and report.** Publish data on jobs displaced, created and redeployed, and on wages, with independent monitoring.
8. **Learn and update.** Revise policies, standards and collective agreements in the light of what happened.

**If you are a worker facing change,** ask early for information, keep a record of what you are told, seek the support of your representatives or a trusted adviser, and use any training rights you have before your situation becomes urgent.

A good transition is not measured by how many tasks machines can do, but by whether the people who used to do them are better off.
