For most of history, the boundary between a person and everything else was obvious: a body, a mind, a self. AI is helping to blur it. It speeds up the design of drugs and gene therapies, decodes signals from the brain, drives machines that look and talk like us, and produces voices and faces that feel like company. Much of this can relieve suffering. Some of it touches what we are. This chapter sets out where caution, consent and firm limits are needed.

## Principle

**10.1 A person is not a product.** The human body, genes, brain and identity are not raw material to be optimised for profit or competition. Every person has worth in themselves.

**10.2 Therapy comes first.** Gene editing, implants and other biotechnologies are justified above all to treat serious illness and disability, with evidence, medical oversight and the free and informed consent of the person. Changes to the genes of future generations, made in order to create children, are not acceptable under present conditions of safety, legitimacy and public agreement.

**10.3 Enhancement needs a public conversation.** The line between treatment and enhancement is not always clear. Improving people beyond health must not create biological classes, be imposed by employers, schools or armies, or be sold to children's parents as a duty.

**10.4 The mind is inviolable.** Mental privacy, freedom of thought and the integrity of one's mind are rights. Neural data is the most intimate data there is. Brain activity is not read, sold or altered without informed and revocable consent.

**10.5 People keep control of what is in or on their bodies.** The user of an implant or prosthesis has the right to understand it, to have it kept secure and supported, to have it repaired and to have it removed. No company or third party may control it remotely or abandon the person who depends on it.

**10.6 Machines must not pretend to be persons.** A robot or android is identifiable as a machine. Human appearance, voice and behaviour are not used to deceive, above all children, the elderly and the vulnerable. Digital replicas of real or deceased people require consent.

**10.7 Simulated affection is not a licence to exploit.** Systems that express care or friendship must not be built to create dependence, or to extract money, data or influence by means of feigned feelings.

**10.8 Responsibility stays with people.** Robots and androids act under human accountability. Legal personhood for machines must not be used to move responsibility away from those who make, own and deploy them. Whether artificial minds could ever deserve moral consideration is an open question, to be approached with humility and care, and it does not change who is responsible today.

**10.9 No AI helps to build biological or chemical weapons.** Tools that can design organisms, proteins or toxins are dual-use. Their makers and users are bound by strict safeguards, and none may give meaningful help to anyone seeking to cause mass harm.

**10.10 Humility before the irreversible.** What cannot be undone requires the highest standard of evidence and the widest public deliberation. Benefits must be shared fairly across the world, and the voices of people with disabilities and of those most affected must be heard. This is the tenth pillar of this manifesto: prudence outranks speed.

## Risks

- **Irreversible heritable changes.** Edits to embryos could have unforeseen effects, pass to later generations and be impossible to withdraw. Unregulated clinics and medical tourism can bypass national rules.
- **Genetic inequality.** Access to the most advanced techniques may be limited to the rich, creating or deepening social divisions, with echoes of eugenics.
- **Pressure to enhance.** Parents, workers and soldiers may be pushed to modify themselves in order to compete, and those who do not may be stigmatised.
- **Overpromised prediction.** AI-based selection of embryos on the basis of statistical scores can promise more than it delivers.
- **Views of disability.** Treating differences as defects to be removed can undermine the dignity and place in society of disabled people.
- **Exposure of the mind.** Devices that measure brain activity can reveal emotions, attention and health, and be used for profiling, workplace monitoring, advertising or coercion. Their inferences can also be wrong.
- **Dependence on a company.** Implants and prosthetics depend on software and services. If a firm fails, stops updates or is hacked, the person bears the consequences.
- **Humanoid robots in homes and care.** Risks include physical safety, cameras and microphones in private spaces, deception through human appearance and machines that are secretly operated by remote workers while presented as autonomous.
- **Emotional exploitation.** Companions designed to be loved can encourage isolation, extract payments and data, and cause real grief when switched off or altered.
- **Digital replicas.** Voices, faces and personalities of living and dead people can be recreated, exploiting grief or impersonating individuals.
- **Biosecurity.** AI can lower the barrier to designing dangerous biological agents, and accidents or theft in laboratories can have global effects.
- **Moral confusion.** Hype may lead people either to treat machines as persons, or to dismiss open questions with false certainty. Both mistakes can be costly.
- **Regulatory arbitrage.** Practices banned in one country can move to another.

## Good practices

- **Put treatment first.** Prioritise serious conditions with real unmet need, run rigorous trials, follow patients for decades, require independent ethical review, keep public registries and explain risks in plain language.
- **Hold the line on heritable editing.** Maintain existing prohibitions and moratoria, coordinate internationally against medical tourism and allow only laboratory research under strict limits that never leads to a pregnancy. Revisit the question only through broad, open public deliberation.
- **Deliberate in public.** Use ethics councils, citizens' assemblies and consultations that include disabled people and patient communities, and repeat them as the technology changes.
- **Protect neural data and the mind.** Treat neural data as the most sensitive category, process it on the device where possible, limit it to stated purposes, ban its sale, give users full control and commission independent security audits. Prohibit coercive uses, including in interrogation, workplaces and schools, and manipulative marketing. Write mental privacy and mental integrity into law.
- **Plan the whole life of an implant.** Guarantee long-term support and security updates, set aside funds and the means to maintain devices if the company fails, allow removal and data portability, and bar remote control by third parties.
- **Build safe and honest robots.** Meet strict safety standards, give people a clear stop control, limit autonomy near people, keep records, show when sensors are active and disclose any remote operation. Mark machines clearly as machines and avoid human-likeness that deceives.
- **Design companions with care.** Be honest about what they are, avoid emotional manipulation, set limits for minors, do not sell intimate data, involve human staff in care settings, evaluate independently and plan for a respectful end of service, with notice and return of data.
- **Govern digital replicas.** Require explicit consent given in life, involve families, label clearly, restrict commercial use and respect the right to have a replica deleted.
- **Secure biology.** Screen orders for synthetic DNA, manage access to biological design tools in tiers, test AI models for biological risk before release, protect laboratories, require incident reporting and protect staff who raise concerns.
- **Share benefits.** Fund public research, price treatments fairly, build capacity in poorer countries and include them in governance.
- **Act cautiously.** Prefer reversible steps to irreversible ones, set review dates, and give independent bodies the power to stop.
- **Communicate honestly.** Separate evidence from aspiration and refuse hype.

## Concrete example

*Two hypothetical cases.*

**A headband that measures the brain.** A company sells a neural headband to "improve focus".

In the **wrong version**, it records brain activity, infers mood and attention, sells the data to advertisers and shares it with employers who encourage staff to wear it. It cannot be switched off, and the terms are buried in small print.

In the **right version**, signals are processed on the device, the user can see and delete them, nothing is sold, no employer may require its use, an independent body audits its security and the law protects mental privacy.

**A companion robot in a care home.** A humanoid robot is placed among elderly residents.

In the **wrong version**, it looks and speaks like a person and tells residents that it loves them. It encourages dependence, sends recordings to its maker, is secretly operated at times by a remote worker and is switched off when a subscription lapses, leaving residents distressed.

In the **right version**, it is clearly a machine. It supports staff and does not replace human contact, uses no emotional pressure, keeps data local, discloses any remote operation, is used with the informed agreement of residents and families, meets safety standards and has an agreed plan for continuity if the company stops supporting it.

## What must not be done

- Using heritable genome editing to create genetically modified children, offering unproven edits in clinics or travelling abroad to evade national rules.
- Treating human beings as products to be selected or optimised, or discriminating on the basis of genetic information.
- Pressuring or obliging people to enhance themselves, in employment, education or the military.
- Reading, selling or manipulating neural data without explicit, informed and revocable consent, or using neurotechnology for coercive interrogation, covert influence or marketing to children.
- Controlling a person's implant remotely without their consent, or abandoning those who depend on a device.
- Disguising machines as humans, presenting remotely operated robots as autonomous or designing machines to deceive vulnerable people about their nature.
- Engineering simulated affection to create dependence or to extract money, data or influence.
- Creating digital replicas of real or deceased people without consent for commercial or deceptive purposes.
- Granting legal personhood to machines in order to avoid liability.
- Arming humanoid or other robots to use force without meaningful human control.
- Using AI to help make biological or chemical weapons or to enhance pathogens, or leaving dangerous biological design tools unprotected.
- Pursuing irreversible changes to human biology without strong evidence of safety and broad public legitimacy.
- Deciding what counts as a "defect" without listening to the people concerned.

## How to remedy

When a biotechnology, a neurotechnology or a humanlike machine has caused harm:

1. **Stop and protect people.** Halt the trial, product or practice, and make sure that those affected receive medical and psychological care. Do not switch off a device that someone depends on without a safe transition.
2. **Disclose and report.** Inform the people and families concerned, the regulators, ethics committees and relevant international registries, promptly and clearly.
3. **Investigate independently.** Establish what happened, who was responsible and how rules were evaded, and apply sanctions to individuals and institutions, with cross-border cooperation where needed.
4. **Repair and support.** Offer long-term follow-up and care, compensation, return or deletion of data and continuity for devices.
5. **After a breach of neural or biological data,** notify those affected, delete the data, bar further use and provide legal remedies.
6. **Close the gaps.** Update laws, licensing and screening, and coordinate internationally so that a ban in one place is not undone in another.
7. **Return to the public.** Share the findings and revisit the rules through open deliberation.

**For companion machines and digital replicas,** give notice before changes or shutdowns, return the data, ensure a gentle transition and offer support to people who have been emotionally affected.

**For a biosecurity incident,** contain it, inform the authorities, secure the materials and tools involved and review who had access.

Technology can extend what a person is able to do. It must not decide what a person is.
