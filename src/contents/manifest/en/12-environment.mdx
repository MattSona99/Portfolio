AI feels weightless. A question is typed, an answer appears. But behind every answer are chips made in factories, minerals taken from the ground, servers housed in vast buildings, power stations that feed them and water that cools them. As AI is used by billions of people, its footprint grows with it. This chapter turns the ninth pillar of this manifesto into practice: the cost of AI to the planet and to people must be declared, reduced and compensated, in that order.

## Principle

**12.1 AI is physical, and its footprint is part of its cost.** Energy, carbon, water, land, minerals and waste are counted across the whole life of a system, from the mine to the landfill, and not only the electricity used to train a model.

**12.2 Declare the cost.** Providers publish clear, standard and independently verified figures on the energy, emissions, water, land and hardware footprint of their models and services, including indirect and supply-chain effects.

**12.3 Use only what is needed.** Efficiency comes first, and the smallest system that does the job is the right one. Some uses are not worth their cost. Growth is not exempt from limits: where demand threatens climate goals or the resources of a community, growth gives way.

**12.4 Power AI with clean energy that is genuinely new.** Data centres should be supplied by low-carbon electricity that adds new capacity, not by taking clean power from others on paper or by prolonging the life of fossil plants. Demand should be flexible, so that it helps the grid rather than straining it.

**12.5 Water belongs first to people and ecosystems.** Data centres and chip factories must not take drinking, farming or ecological water from those who need it, above all in regions already under water stress.

**12.6 Materials must be obtained with justice.** The minerals in chips and servers must come from sources free of forced and child labour, with the consent of the communities concerned and a duty to restore what is damaged.

**12.7 Design for repair, reuse and recycling.** Hardware is built to last, to be repaired, refurbished and recycled, and electronic waste is never exported to places that cannot handle it safely.

**12.8 Communities have a say.** People who live near data centres, factories and mines are informed and consulted, share in the benefits and are not made to pay for the infrastructure through higher bills, noise or pollution.

**12.9 Climate benefits must be earned.** AI may help the planet, for example by improving energy systems, monitoring emissions and discovering materials. Those claims must be measured and weighed against the system's own footprint, and they never excuse it.

## Risks

- **Surging electricity demand.** Rapid growth strains power grids, may delay the closure of fossil plants, encourages new gas capacity and can push up prices for households.
- **Hidden carbon.** Emissions come not only from electricity but from building data centres and manufacturing hardware, which are often left out of the accounts.
- **Water stress.** Cooling uses large amounts of water, directly and through power generation. Chip manufacturing is water-intensive too. Many facilities are located where water is already scarce.
- **Land and local pollution.** Sprawling sites, noise, heat and diesel backup generators affect nearby communities and habitats.
- **Destructive mining.** Lithium, cobalt, gallium, rare earths and other minerals are often extracted where environmental protection is weak and human rights are at risk, mostly in poorer countries.
- **E-waste.** Specialised hardware is replaced after a few years, and toxic waste can end up in informal recycling, often in countries that did not benefit from the technology.
- **Rebound effect.** Efficiency gains are cancelled out when cheaper AI leads to far more use, so total consumption still rises.
- **Opacity and greenwashing.** Companies may report only favourable figures, leave out indirect water or hardware, or claim "100% renewable" through paper certificates.
- **The weight of use.** Once millions of people use a system, running it can count for more than training it.
- **Unequal burdens.** The benefits of AI concentrate in a few places, while mining, water use, pollution and the effects of climate change fall elsewhere, and on the most vulnerable.
- **Fossil lock-in.** Infrastructure built for AI may be used as a reason to keep high-emission energy for decades.
- **Frivolous use at scale.** Huge volumes of trivial generated content consume resources for little value.

## Good practices

- **Measure and publish.** Report standard figures per model or service for energy, emissions (by location and by market method), water (direct and indirect), land and the embodied footprint of hardware. Have them verified by third parties and give researchers and regulators the data needed for independent assessment.
- **Design for efficiency.** Use smaller, specialised models where they are enough, and apply techniques such as distillation, quantisation, caching and batching. Choose efficient chips and efficient defaults, avoid unnecessary generation and measure performance per unit of energy.
- **Ask whether to build it.** Carry out an environmental assessment before large training runs and large deployments, and decline low-value uses.
- **Use clean power well.** Contract for new renewable capacity, match consumption hour by hour with carbon-free supply, shift flexible workloads to times and places where clean energy is abundant, add storage, reuse waste heat for buildings, and keep backup generators to a minimum and as clean as possible.
- **Protect water.** Choose sites after a water-risk assessment, use closed-loop, air or liquid cooling and recycled or non-drinking water, report water use, prepare drought plans and invest in local water resilience.
- **Extend the life of hardware.** Keep equipment in service longer, refurbish and resell it, use modular and repairable designs, take products back, use certified recyclers, never export e-waste and design for disassembly.
- **Source minerals responsibly.** Apply supply-chain due diligence and traceability, ban forced and child labour, require the free, prior and informed consent of affected communities, restore mined land and increase recycling to reduce new extraction. Build fair partnerships that let producing countries share in the value.
- **Clean up chip manufacturing.** Recycle water, manage chemicals safely, use renewable power and report the results.
- **Involve communities.** Conduct environmental impact assessments with public hearings, make benefit agreements public, and ensure that large new loads pay the full cost of the grid upgrades they cause, so that household bills are protected.
- **Regulate sensibly.** Require environmental reporting, set efficiency standards, plan data centres together with energy, water and land use, and align incentives so that the least wasteful options win.
- **Choose lighter as a user.** Pick the smallest model that does the job, avoid pointless regeneration, delete data you no longer need, ask suppliers for footprint data and include AI in your organisation's sustainability reporting.
- **Use AI for the planet, and prove it.** Apply it to grid management, industrial efficiency, leak detection, climate modelling and biodiversity monitoring, measure the net benefit and publish the results.
- **Fund research** on more efficient algorithms and hardware and on better life-cycle assessment.

## Concrete example

*Two hypothetical cases.*

**A new data centre.** A company plans a large data centre in a drought-prone region.

In the **wrong version**, the site is chosen for cheap land and tax breaks. It cools with drinking water, and the extra demand keeps a coal plant open longer. It relies on diesel for backup and on renewable certificates bought elsewhere on paper. The footprint is not disclosed, the community learns about the project from rumours, household bills rise and the servers are replaced every few years and sent abroad as waste.

In the **right version**, the site is chosen after an assessment of environmental and water risk, in a region with abundant clean power. It uses closed-loop or recycled-water cooling and commissions new renewable capacity and storage, matching its use hour by hour. Waste heat warms local buildings, flexible workloads follow the supply of clean energy, and the company publishes its energy, water and carbon figures. The community was consulted and shares in the benefits, the company pays for grid upgrades and hardware is refurbished and then recycled through certified channels.

**The right-sized model.** A team needs to sort support emails into categories.

In the **wrong version**, it uses the largest general-purpose model available for every message, because it is convenient.

In the **right version**, it uses a small specialised model that does the job as well, at a fraction of the energy, and reserves the large model for the rare complex cases.

## What must not be done

- Hiding or understating the footprint, reporting only convenient figures, or leaving out indirect water and hardware.
- Claiming to be "100% renewable" through paper certificates while drawing on fossil power.
- Building facilities that take drinking or farming water from people who need it, push up household bills or delay the closure of fossil plants, without consent and fair compensation.
- Using AI growth as an excuse to lock in new fossil infrastructure.
- Running giant models for trivial purposes when lighter ones would do, or setting wasteful defaults.
- Sourcing minerals through forced or child labour, unsafe mining or the seizure of communities' land without their consent.
- Dumping or burning e-waste, shipping it to poorer countries, or designing hardware that cannot be repaired.
- Greenwashing: citing "AI for climate" to excuse a large footprint, or claiming benefits that have not been measured.
- Taking decisions about siting and permits in secret, without those who live nearby.
- Silencing researchers, workers and communities who raise environmental concerns.
- Treating efficiency gains as a licence to expand consumption without limit.

## How to remedy

When AI infrastructure or practice has caused environmental harm:

1. **Stop the damage.** Reduce the load, switch to an alternative water source, pause expansion, shut down polluting backup generators and restore supplies to the community first.
2. **Measure and disclose.** Publish verified data, including indirect effects, and submit it to independent audit.
3. **Repair.** Restore water bodies, remediate pollution and mined land, clean up waste and compensate affected communities and farmers.
4. **Reduce before offsetting.** Compensation is for what cannot be avoided, must be real and additional, and cannot replace cuts.
5. **Redesign.** Move to more efficient models, a better site or cooling method, longer hardware life and new clean capacity, and operate flexibly.
6. **Fix governance.** Attach environmental conditions to permits, set penalties and mandatory reporting, give communities a role in oversight, make permits revocable and protect whistleblowers.
7. **Share the lessons.** Set binding targets, publish what was learned and review them regularly.

**As a user or an organisation,** choose lighter tools, avoid waste, ask suppliers for their figures and support policies that require transparency.

A technology that helps us understand the planet cannot be built by consuming it. Its cost must be declared, reduced and compensated.
