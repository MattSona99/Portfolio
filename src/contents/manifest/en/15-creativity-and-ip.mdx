Generative AI learned to write, paint, compose and code from what people had already made. Now it produces work that competes in the same markets as the people it learned from. Many see a tool that opens creativity to millions. Many creators see their life's work taken without asking and sold back to the public as a product. Both views contain truth. This chapter looks for a way to protect creators, culture and the shared commons of knowledge at the same time.

## Principle

**15.1 Human creativity has worth in itself.** Art, writing, music, design, craft and science express human experience. AI can extend them, and must not erase them.

**15.2 Creators are not raw material.** People whose work is used to build AI are entitled to consent, credit and fair compensation or, at the very least, to a simple and effective way to say no.

**15.3 How data is obtained matters.** Training on pirated, stolen or illegally accessed works, or on material taken in breach of clear terms and licences, is not acceptable. The lawfulness of acquisition is not optional.

**15.4 Rights and the commons are balanced.** Copyright serves society as well as authors. Libraries, education, research, criticism, parody and accessibility are part of that service. Rules for AI must neither enclose the public domain and block legitimate research, nor allow wholesale appropriation of creative work. Clear and narrow exceptions give both sides legal certainty.

**15.5 Use is transparent.** Developers disclose what kinds of protected works, and from which sources, were used for training. Creators can find out whether their work was used and can object.

**15.6 Authorship is human and honest.** Protection follows meaningful human creative contribution. AI involvement is declared where it matters, such as in contests, publishing and credits, and nobody claims to be the sole human author of what a machine essentially produced.

**15.7 Identity is not a style sheet.** A person's name, voice, face and signature work are protected. Imitating a living creator in order to pass as them, to mislead the public or to undercut them is not acceptable.

**15.8 Creative livelihoods deserve protection.** Creative workers are entitled to fair terms, collective representation and a fair share of the value that AI draws from culture. The market must not be flooded in ways that push human work into poverty.

**15.9 Culture belongs to communities.** Traditional and indigenous knowledge and cultural expressions are used only with the consent of their communities, and with a fair share of the benefits.

## Risks

- **Taking without consent or pay.** Creators see their work used to build products that compete with them, with no notice, credit or payment.
- **Pirated sources.** Datasets assembled from illegal copies reward the theft of creative work.
- **Substitution.** Generated images, illustrations, translations, voices, music and text can replace commissions and lower prices in the very markets the models learned from.
- **Flooding and fraud.** Marketplaces and platforms can be swamped by synthetic content. Fake books or songs can be attached to real artists' names, and AI work can be sold as handmade.
- **Legal uncertainty.** Courts and countries disagree, creators and developers cannot be sure of their position, and litigation is slow and costly. Large companies can afford it, while individual creators often cannot.
- **Memorisation.** Models can reproduce protected passages, images or code almost verbatim.
- **Cloned voices and faces.** Performers, living and dead, can be imitated, and endorsements can be faked.
- **Disputes over authorship.** It can be unclear who owns an output, who is credited, and what amount of human input is needed for protection. People may falsely claim sole authorship.
- **Open-source erosion.** Code generators may reproduce licensed code without respecting its conditions, and maintainers may be burdened by machine-generated contributions.
- **Over-enclosure.** Overbroad restrictions could choke research, libraries, archives and criticism, and push culture behind ever more paywalls.
- **Benefits that do not reach creators.** Licensing deals can concentrate payments in large rights holders and intermediaries, and favour incumbents who can afford them.
- **Cultural homogenisation.** Average outputs from the same few systems can flatten diversity of style and voice.
- **Appropriation of traditional knowledge.** Communities' songs, patterns and stories can be taken without consent.
- **False authenticity.** Work presented as human-made when it is not erodes trust in credentials, craft and scholarship.

## Good practices

- **Make consent, credit and compensation the standard.** Develop licensing frameworks, and require opt-in for especially sensitive categories. Provide opt-out systems that are simple, universal, machine-readable and honoured. Use collective licensing and management, with transparent rates and with payments that actually reach individual creators.
- **Source lawfully.** Record the provenance of data, exclude pirated material, respect terms of use and technical rights signals, keep an audit trail, remove works on request where feasible and settle claims in good faith.
- **Disclose.** Publish a summary of the content used for training and give creators tools to check whether their work is included.
- **Build output safeguards.** Reduce regurgitation of protected material, offer provenance tags and content credentials for generated media, and do not generate work in the name of a living artist.
- **Protect likeness and voice.** Require consent, written contracts, limits on scope and duration, payment and protection for estates for any digital replica. Provide fast takedown routes against deceptive impersonation.
- **Label.** Mark AI-generated or AI-assisted works in marketplaces and competitions, keep separate categories where it matters, verify claims of "human-made" and remove fraudulent uploads quickly.
- **Support creators.** Avoid contracts that demand all rights, including training rights, as a condition of work. Support collective bargaining, transparency in commissions, public funding for culture, and funds that share AI revenue with creators and cultural institutions. Help emerging artists in particular.
- **Protect the commons.** Maintain exceptions for research, libraries, education, criticism, parody and accessibility. Support public-interest datasets and digitisation by libraries, honour open licences and build licence-aware tools for developers.
- **Clarify human contribution.** Give practical guidance on how much human creative input is needed, encourage creators to document their process, and use contracts to settle who owns what between users, tools and clients.
- **Credit and cite.** Researchers, journalists and authors should state how they used AI, cite their sources and not use AI to launder other people's work.
- **Respect traditional knowledge.** Follow community protocols, seek prior consent and agree on benefit-sharing.
- **As a user, respect others' rights.** Do not ask a system to imitate a living artist for commercial gain, check the licences of what you use, credit sources, and commission and buy human work.
- **Strengthen the system.** Pass clear laws, coordinate internationally, offer fast and low-cost dispute resolution for small creators and allow collective redress.
- **Use AI in the service of creators.** Develop tools for provenance, detection of infringement, translation and accessibility, on fair terms.

## Concrete example

*Two hypothetical cases.*

**An illustrator's portfolio.** An illustrator discovers that an image generator learned from her portfolio. A publisher has used it to produce a cover "in her style" instead of commissioning her, and a stranger is selling AI images signed with her name.

In the **wrong version**, nobody can tell her whether her work was used, she has no opt-out and no payment. The platform leaves the listing in her name online, the publisher says nothing about how the cover was made, and any court case would take years and money she does not have.

In the **right version**, the developer has published a summary of its sources and honoured her opt-out, or pays her through a collective licence. The publisher marks the cover as AI-assisted and commissions her for the main work. The platform removes the false listing quickly, and she has an affordable route to resolve disputes.

**A novel and a prize.** A novelist uses AI to help draft part of a book and enters it for a literary prize.

In the **wrong version**, she claims to be the sole author, although the rules forbid AI-generated text, and she wins.

In the **right version**, she declares how she used AI as the rules require, and keeps her drafts and notes to show her own creative contribution.

## What must not be done

- Training on pirated, stolen or illegally accessed works, or on material taken in breach of clear terms, technical restrictions or opt-outs.
- Ignoring creators' requests to know whether and how their work was used, or refusing any disclosure about sources.
- Using generative systems to imitate a living creator's name, voice, signature work or likeness in order to pass as them, to mislead the public or to undercut them.
- Selling AI-generated work as hand-made or human-made, entering it in contests that forbid it or falsely claiming sole authorship.
- Cloning the voices or faces of performers, living or dead, without consent and fair terms.
- Imposing contracts that force creators to hand over all rights, including for AI training, without fair payment.
- Flooding marketplaces with synthetic content to deceive customers, to collect royalties fraudulently or to impersonate artists.
- Reproducing protected works verbatim through models, or stripping credits, metadata and licences.
- Violating open-source licences, for instance by reproducing licensed code without respecting its conditions.
- Enclosing the public domain, or using AI rules to block legitimate research, libraries, education, criticism and parody.
- Appropriating traditional or indigenous cultural expressions without the consent of the communities concerned.
- Presenting AI-written text or invented references as scholarly work.

## How to remedy

When creative work has been used or imitated unfairly:

1. **Stop and take down.** Remove infringing or deceptive content, such as impersonation and fraud, stop further use and preserve the evidence.
2. **Inform and credit.** Tell creators that their work was used, correct credits and label the content accurately.
3. **Compensate.** Pay licence fees or damages, make sure that collective payments reach individual creators and settle claims in good faith.
4. **Fix the sourcing.** Audit the training data, remove or retrain where feasible, delete on request, honour opt-outs from now on and document the changes.
5. **Provide accessible dispute resolution.** Offer fast and affordable procedures for small creators, collective actions, mediation and legal aid.
6. **Repair harm to identity.** Remove clones and false endorsements, compensate those harmed, protect estates and publish a clear correction.
7. **Update rules and contracts.** Improve transparency, labelling, opt-out systems and collective licensing, negotiating with creators' representatives.
8. **Keep learning.** Consult creators and communities, and revisit the rules as courts and laws evolve.

**If you are a creator whose work was used,** document what you find, check whether an opt-out channel or registry exists, contact the developer or platform, and seek help from collective management organisations, creators' associations and legal clinics. Consider joining collective action.

**If you used AI in a way that may infringe someone's rights,** stop, remove the material, correct the credits, contact the rights holder and ask for advice.

Culture is a conversation across generations. AI may join it, but only on terms that those who spoke first can accept.
