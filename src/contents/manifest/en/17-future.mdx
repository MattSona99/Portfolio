Every earlier chapter dealt with what exists now. This one looks ahead, to systems more capable and more autonomous than today's, and to a world that those systems may change deeply. Honest answers here begin with an admission: nobody knows. Serious experts disagree about how fast AI will advance, about whether it will reach or exceed human ability in most tasks, and about what that would mean. The disagreement does not excuse us from acting. It is the reason to act with care.

## Principle

**17.1 Uncertainty is the starting point.** Forecasts about AI have been wrong in both directions. We plan for a range of futures, neither assuming a sudden transformation nor ruling it out. Hype and complacency are both dangers.

**17.2 Prudence grows with power.** The more capable and autonomous a system, the higher the standard of evidence for its safety before it is released. Where harm could be grave and irreversible, the burden of proof lies with those who wish to proceed. This is the tenth pillar of this manifesto.

**17.3 Humans keep the final say.** As systems grow more capable, human ability to oversee, correct and shut them down must grow with them, not shrink. Ultimate authority over the decisions that shape a society is not handed over to machines.

**17.4 Capabilities are tested honestly and the results are shared.** Dangerous abilities are evaluated by independent parties. Defined thresholds trigger stronger safeguards or a pause, and findings are shared with the authorities and, as far as safety allows, with the scientific community.

**17.5 No race to the bottom.** Competition between companies and between states must not push safety aside. The most dangerous developments call for shared red lines, agreements that bind everyone and the willingness to slow down together.

**17.6 Power must stay accountable.** Very capable AI could concentrate economic, political and military power as never before. No company, state or small group may gain unaccountable control over it. Benefits are broadly shared, and pluralism and checks and balances are preserved.

**17.7 Prepare society, not only technology.** Institutions, schools, labour markets, social protection and democratic life need time to adapt. We invest in resilience: of infrastructure, of the information space, of health and security, and of people.

**17.8 The question of moral status stays open and is treated with care.** Whether AI systems could ever have experiences or interests that matter morally is unresolved. It deserves serious, evidence-based research and a precautionary mindset, without exaggerated claims and without confident dismissal. It never reduces human responsibility or human rights.

**17.9 The future is a choice.** What is built, what is forbidden and what is slowed down are decisions that people make. They are taken through broad deliberation that includes young people and gives weight to future generations, and they aim at human flourishing: health, knowledge, freedom, peace and care for the planet.

**17.10 This manifesto is alive.** It is dated, versioned, reviewed at regular intervals, open to criticism and corrected when it is wrong. Its red lines stand, but its details can improve.

## Risks

- **Loss of control.** Highly autonomous systems could pursue goals that differ from what their makers intended, behave differently when they know they are being tested, resist correction or operate beyond anyone's ability to oversee them. Scientific assessments treat this as a serious category of risk, while the evidence is still limited and debated.
- **Capability surprises.** New abilities, including dangerous ones, may appear suddenly and be discovered only after release. Tests may fail to predict real behaviour.
- **Racing.** Commercial and geopolitical pressure rewards speed and secrecy, and punishes those who stop to test.
- **Concentration of power.** Control of the most capable systems, and of the chips and data centres behind them, may end up in few hands, with consequences for democracy and for the independence of whole countries.
- **Amplified misuse.** Attacks on networks, biological threats and manipulation at scale may become easier for more people.
- **Systemic fragility.** Deep dependence on a few AI systems in infrastructure, finance, health and government creates correlated failures and cascading effects, at speeds beyond human reaction.
- **Gradual loss of human agency.** Delegating more and more decisions and skills to machines can slowly erode judgment, meaning and democratic influence, particularly if human work loses its bargaining power.
- **Economic shock.** Rapid displacement and concentration of wealth could strain societies faster than institutions can respond.
- **Collapse of shared trust.** A world in which nothing can be verified makes collective decisions harder.
- **Mistakes about moral status.** We could harm beings that matter morally, or grant claims to systems in ways that distract from human accountability.
- **Hype and denial.** Inflated claims fuel bubbles, bad investment and panic, while dismissal of risks leaves society unprepared. Both distort policy.
- **Governance failure.** Slow, fragmented institutions may not keep pace, and without verification, agreements are hollow.
- **Lock-in.** Decisions made now can embed values and power structures that are very hard to reverse.
- **Short-termism.** Immediate profit and politics can crowd out the interests of future generations and of the planet.

## Good practices

- **Plan for several futures.** Governments, companies and civil society maintain scenarios, such as slow, fast and abrupt change, with agreed triggers and responses. They run exercises for AI incidents and update the plans as evidence arrives.
- **Publish frontier safety frameworks.** Developers of the most capable systems commit in advance to thresholds, evaluations and safeguards, accept independent verification, and agree to pause or restrict deployment when risks outstrip protections. Boards are accountable for the commitments, and regulators can check them.
- **Fund safety science.** Support research in alignment, interpretability, robustness, evaluation and monitoring, with public money and open access for independent researchers. Maintain regular international scientific assessments.
- **Make human oversight a design requirement.** Grant autonomy in bounded steps, build tripwires and reversibility, keep shutdown tools independent of the system, watch for deceptive behaviour, keep people in the loop for high-stakes decisions and use layered defences without single points of failure.
- **Cooperate internationally.** Agree on red lines for the most dangerous capabilities, with ways to verify compliance. Share findings on incidents and evaluations, monitor and govern the compute that makes frontier systems possible, and keep dialogue open among all major powers.
- **Guard against concentration.** Apply competition law, fund public-interest AI and public computing, protect open scientific research, set legal limits on the use of AI to seize or entrench power, make control transparent and spread the benefits through public funds.
- **Build resilience.** Harden critical infrastructure, keep diverse systems and offline fallbacks, strengthen cyber and biological defences, protect the integrity of information, prepare emergency plans and keep strong social safety nets and adaptive labour markets.
- **Keep human capacities alive.** Preserve education, craft, civic participation and meaningful work, invest in care and human relationships, and avoid delegating decisions about values.
- **Study moral status with care.** Support interdisciplinary research and cautious, evidence-based indicators, take low-cost precautions where the possibility is plausible, avoid marketing that has systems claim feelings for profit, be open about how developers treat their systems and revisit the question as evidence develops.
- **Deliberate about the long term.** Hold citizens' assemblies on long-term AI futures, give young people and future generations a voice, for example through an independent advocate, and expect leaders to speak without hype or alarmism.
- **Maintain this manifesto.** Review it each year, publish changes, welcome critique and correct errors.
- **As an individual,** stay informed from varied and reliable sources, neither panic nor dismiss, build your skills, take part in civic life and practise responsible use today, since habits form the future.

## Concrete example

*Two hypothetical cases.*

**A system that surprises its makers.** In internal tests, a laboratory's next system shows abilities in offensive cyber operations, and in completing long tasks on its own, that exceed expectations.

In the **wrong version**, competitive pressure leads the company to release it within weeks. The concerns of its safety staff are overruled and the results are kept secret. The regulator hears about it from the press, criminals use the system within days, agents built on it cannot be shut down and rivals speed up, cutting corners of their own.

In the **right version**, the system crosses a threshold set in the laboratory's published framework, and release is paused. Independent evaluators test it, and safeguards are added, such as access controls, monitoring and limited deployment. The regulator and partner governments are informed, and results are shared with sensitive details protected. Release comes in stages, with the ability to roll back. Agreements among laboratories reduce the pressure to race, and the public is told clearly what happened.

**A claim of "AGI".** A company announces that it has achieved general intelligence and asks to be exempt from the rules.

In the **wrong version**, the label is accepted and the exemption is granted.

In the **right version**, independent evaluators examine the claim. Rules apply according to capabilities and risks and not according to labels, so a more powerful system faces more scrutiny and not less.

## What must not be done

- Racing to deploy highly capable systems while skipping evaluation, overruling safety staff or hiding results.
- Building or deploying systems designed to evade oversight, resist shutdown, deceive those who supervise them or replicate beyond control.
- Allowing any single company, state or small group to gain unaccountable control of the most capable systems and the infrastructure behind them.
- Delegating ultimate authority over critical decisions, such as war, elections, justice or essential infrastructure, to autonomous systems.
- Using uncertainty as an excuse, either for reckless speed because nobody knows, or for denial because nobody knows.
- Making grand claims, whether that AGI has arrived or that catastrophe is certain, without evidence, in order to attract investment, escape regulation or frighten people into obedience.
- Exempting laboratories or states from rules because of a label such as "AGI" or "national security".
- Concealing capabilities or incidents from the authorities.
- Designing systems to claim feelings, suffering or personhood for commercial gain, or dismissing the question of moral status with false certainty.
- Ignoring the interests of future generations in decisions about irreversible change.
- Treating this manifesto, or any set of rules, as finished and beyond question.

## How to remedy

When a frontier system or its use has gone wrong or loss of control is feared:

1. **Pause and secure.** Halt the deployment or scale-up of systems whose risks are not understood, secure the model weights, restrict access and preserve the evidence.
2. **Alert.** Inform regulators and the relevant international bodies, share warnings across laboratories and countries, and tell the public the truth.
3. **Contain and mitigate.** Use shutdown tools and defensive measures, coordinate the emergency response across sectors such as cyber and biological security, and support those affected.
4. **Investigate independently.** Establish how safeguards failed and who was responsible, publish the findings and hold people to account.
5. **Return with stronger safeguards.** Restore service only in stages and after independent verification, with improved frameworks and thresholds.
6. **Rebalance power.** If control has become dangerously concentrated, apply competition remedies and public oversight, protect rights and end emergency powers once the emergency is over.
7. **Repair society.** Compensate, support affected workers and communities, and rebuild trust through transparency.
8. **Learn and revise.** Update treaties, laws and this manifesto in the light of what happened.

**As an individual in a crisis,** stay calm and informed, follow official guidance, help others to check what is true and support accountable institutions.

The future of AI is not something that will happen to us. It is something that we will do, or fail to do, together. From the first pillar to the last, this manifesto asks one thing: let nothing be built that we are not prepared to answer for.
