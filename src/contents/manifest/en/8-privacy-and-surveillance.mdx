AI runs on data, and the data it runs on is often about us: our faces, voices, movements, messages, health, habits and relationships. It can also infer what we never said. The same techniques that help a doctor or a translator can build a profile of a whole population, or follow a person through a city. This chapter defends a simple idea: there must be a part of life that remains one's own.

## Principle

**8.1 Your data is yours.** Every person has the right to know what is held about them, to see it, correct it, delete it, take it elsewhere and object to how it is used.

**8.2 Consent must be real.** It must be informed, specific, freely given and easy to withdraw. It is not buried in long terms, bundled with unrelated services or obtained through design tricks. Children are owed special protection.

**8.3 Collect less, keep less, protect more.** Data is gathered only for a stated purpose, kept only as long as needed and secured against loss and theft. "Just in case" is not a purpose. Those who hold data must protect it, and tell the truth when they fail.

**8.4 Public does not mean free for the taking.** A face, a voice or a post that can be seen online is still a part of a person. Its visibility does not give anyone the right to harvest it, profile it or use it without regard for the person it comes from.

**8.5 Inference is intrusion.** AI can deduce health conditions, beliefs, orientation, finances or intentions from apparently harmless data. Inferring a sensitive characteristic deserves the same protection as collecting it, and must not be used to discriminate.

**8.6 Biometrics are special.** A face, a voice, a gait or a fingerprint cannot be changed like a password. Identifying people on a mass scale in public spaces, recognising emotions at work or in school, and sorting people by sensitive characteristics from their bodies are practices that a free society does not accept.

**8.7 Surveillance requires law, necessity and oversight.** Any watching of people by governments or companies needs a clear legal basis, a real need, proportion, a limit in time, independent authorisation, transparency and a remedy for abuse. The innocent are not put under permanent observation.

**8.8 The right to be forgotten, and to say no.** People can have their data erased, can refuse to be profiled and can object to its use to train AI, and these rights are honoured in practice and not only on paper.

**8.9 Privacy protects the vulnerable.** Children, minorities, dissidents, journalists, whistleblowers and survivors of abuse often depend on privacy for their safety. Secure and private communication is a condition of free expression, not a threat to it.

## Risks

- **Collection without limit.** Data is gathered in bulk, combined across sources and traded by brokers, and profiles of people are bought and sold without their knowledge.
- **Training on personal data.** Faces, voices, messages and documents can be scraped without consent, and models can memorise and later reveal personal information.
- **Revealing inference.** Sensitive traits can be guessed from shopping, location, typing or photographs, and "anonymous" data can often be linked back to a person.
- **Face recognition at scale.** Errors and biases lead to misidentification, while the mere presence of the technology discourages people from attending a protest, a clinic or a place of worship. Face search tools make stalking and exposure easier.
- **Pseudo-scientific emotion reading.** Systems claim to read feelings, honesty or aptitude from a face or a voice, with little scientific support.
- **Function creep.** Data collected for one purpose is later used for another, such as emergency data for policing or health data for pricing.
- **State and commercial surveillance.** Mass monitoring, social scoring and the export of surveillance tools to regimes that use them for repression.
- **Always-on devices.** Microphones, cameras, wearables, cars, toys and location services record the intimate details of homes and children.
- **Assistants with memory.** AI agents with broad access to messages, files and calendars create a single point of failure, and invite people to confide in systems whose use of that information they cannot see.
- **Breaches.** Large stores of sensitive data are an attractive target, and leaks of health, identity or location data cannot be undone.
- **Consent fatigue and dark patterns.** People are pushed to click "accept" without reading, because refusing is made difficult.
- **Stalkerware and abuse.** Covert tracking and monitoring tools are used against partners, children and employees.
- **Chilling effects.** When people know they are watched, they stop speaking, associating and exploring, and democracy loses its freedom of thought.

## Good practices

- **Design for privacy from the start.** Make the most protective setting the default, run an impact assessment before deploying any system that touches personal data, and ask first whether the data is needed at all.
- **Make consent honest.** Explain in plain language, offer separate choices, make withdrawal as easy as agreement and avoid design that nudges people to accept. For children, use age-appropriate explanations and involve their guardians.
- **Minimise and expire.** Collect only what is needed, set short retention periods with automatic deletion, and process data on the device or in federated form where possible. Test anonymisation against attempts at re-identification.
- **Govern training data.** Record where data came from and on what legal basis, honour opt-outs and deletion requests as far as technically possible, test models for memorisation and leakage, and prevent systems from revealing personal information in their outputs.
- **Control inference.** Do not use inferred sensitive traits to make decisions about people, and audit systems for this.
- **Handle biometrics with the greatest restraint.** Avoid them where an alternative exists. Where they are used, require a strict legal basis, test accuracy across groups, have a human verify matches, offer a non-biometric option and do not retain templates longer than necessary. Do not deploy tools that identify strangers from their faces.
- **Give users control over assistants.** Let people see, edit and delete what an assistant remembers, switch memory off, keep different contexts separate, and ask before accessing sensitive data.
- **Bind state surveillance.** Require a clear law, proof of necessity and proportion, independent or judicial authorisation, time limits, public reporting, notification of those affected whenever possible and effective redress. Control the export of surveillance technology.
- **Secure what you hold.** Use strong encryption, restrict access, keep audit logs and notify breaches promptly. Protect secure communications for everyone.
- **Empower individuals.** Review privacy settings, think before sharing images of other people and especially of children, use privacy tools and exercise your rights of access, deletion and objection.
- **Strengthen oversight.** Give independent data-protection authorities the powers and resources to act, allow collective redress and support independent privacy research.

## Concrete example

*Two hypothetical cases.*

**Cameras in a shop.** A retailer wants to reduce theft.

In the **wrong version**, it installs facial recognition at every entrance. Every visitor is scanned, faces are stored indefinitely and watchlists are shared with other shops. An innocent customer is wrongly flagged and banned, with no explanation and no way to appeal.

In the **right version**, the retailer first tries less intrusive measures, such as store layout, staffing and tagging goods. It does not identify people by their faces. Where any monitoring exists, it is signposted, footage is deleted quickly, a human reviews incidents and there is a clear route to contest a decision.

**An AI assistant with memory.** A company launches an assistant that remembers its users.

In the **wrong version**, it silently remembers everything, including a user's mention of a health condition, uses it to target advertising and offers no way to delete it.

In the **right version**, the memory can be seen, edited and erased by the user, it can be switched off, sensitive topics are not retained without a clear choice, nothing is used for advertising and the data stays under the user's control.

## What must not be done

- Mass surveillance of the innocent, or the identification of people in public spaces on a mass scale by facial recognition.
- Scraping faces, voices or personal data in bulk to build identification databases.
- Using emotion recognition at work or in school, or claiming to infer character, orientation, beliefs or criminality from a face or voice.
- Inferring sensitive characteristics from biometrics or behaviour in order to sort, rank or exclude people.
- Scoring citizens on their general behaviour.
- Collecting data "just in case", using it for undisclosed purposes or selling it to brokers.
- Obtaining consent by deception or design tricks, or making a service conditional on data it does not need.
- Training AI on personal data without a lawful basis, ignoring deletion and objection requests, or allowing systems to disclose personal data in their outputs.
- Using stalkerware or covertly tracking partners, children or employees.
- Exporting surveillance tools to regimes that will use them to repress people.
- Hiding breaches or delaying notification to those affected.

## How to remedy

When privacy has been violated:

1. **Stop and contain.** Halt the collection or the use at issue, restrict access and freeze further sharing.
2. **Secure and assess.** If there has been a breach, establish what data was involved and who is affected, close the weakness and revoke compromised keys and credentials.
3. **Notify.** Inform affected people and the competent authority promptly and clearly, saying what happened and what they can do, as the law requires.
4. **Delete and correct.** Erase data that was collected unlawfully, including from derived datasets and training sets, and where feasible retrain or remove its influence from models. Document what was done.
5. **Support and compensate.** Offer protection against identity theft and other follow-on harm, and compensation for the damage.
6. **Review and hold to account.** Carry out an independent review and make decision-makers answer for the failure.
7. **Redesign.** Reduce data, strengthen safeguards, improve consent and shorten retention.

**If you are the person affected,** ask the organisation for access to your data and for its deletion, object to its use, and complain to the data-protection authority if you are not satisfied. Change passwords, keep a record of what happened and, if you are being tracked or your images are being abused, seek help from specialised support services and, where appropriate, the police.

Privacy is not about having something to hide. It is about having something to protect: the freedom to think, to speak, to make mistakes and to change.
