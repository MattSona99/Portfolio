Every chapter of this manifesto depends on something that is not technical: who decides, who checks and who answers. Principles without institutions are wishes. This chapter describes the machinery that makes the others real: laws and standards, regulators and courts, auditors and researchers, and a public that can take part. It does not claim that one design is perfect. It asks that the design be legitimate, effective and open to correction.

## Principle

**16.1 Rules need democratic legitimacy.** The people affected by AI have a say in the rules that govern it. They are not written only by developers or by a few powerful states, and they serve the public interest.

**16.2 Red lines are fixed.** Some uses of AI are unacceptable everywhere, whatever the commercial or political advantage: machines taking life without human control, social scoring, mass biometric surveillance, deceptive manipulation, the exploitation of children and help in making weapons of mass harm. The law names them and allows no exceptions of convenience.

**16.3 Rules are proportionate to risk.** The greater the possible harm, the stricter the duties. Low-risk uses are left free, and small actors and researchers are not crushed by obligations meant for giants. Neither over-regulation nor neglect is a virtue.

**16.4 Binding rules, not only promises.** Voluntary commitments and codes of conduct are welcome, but they do not replace enforceable law. Rules without the power to investigate, suspend and sanction are decoration.

**16.5 Independent scrutiny.** Those who build or sell AI do not alone judge its safety and fairness. Independent auditors, regulators and researchers are given access and legal protection to test systems and to publish what they find.

**16.6 Clear accountability and redress.** Responsibility is allocated along the whole chain, from developers to those who deploy and use systems. Anyone harmed can obtain an explanation, a remedy and compensation, without having to prove what only the system's owner can see.

**16.7 Openness about incidents and risks.** Serious incidents are reported, high-risk and public-sector systems are registered, and people who raise concerns are protected. Safety is learned collectively.

**16.8 The public takes part.** Citizens, workers, young people, communities and civil society have real ways to be informed, to be consulted and to influence decisions, and the means to use them.

**16.9 Cooperation across borders.** AI crosses frontiers, so rules must be coordinated: minimum shared standards, mutual recognition, shared scientific assessment and support for countries with fewer resources. Every state, large or small, has a seat.

**16.10 Rules that learn.** Governance is reviewed regularly against evidence. It can move quickly when a new risk appears, and give enough certainty for people to plan. Where the risk is grave and irreversible, prudence comes before speed.

## Risks

- **Governance lag.** Technology moves faster than law, regulators lack technical expertise, and evidence arrives only after harm has been done.
- **Regulatory capture.** Industry lobbying, revolving doors and standards bodies dominated by large firms can bend rules to the interests of those regulated.
- **Fragmentation.** Conflicting rules between countries and states raise costs, invite forum shopping and a race to the bottom, and leave some regions without protection. Geopolitical rivalry may block shared rules altogether.
- **Paper compliance and ethics-washing.** Checklists, pledges and self-certification can produce the look of responsibility without the substance.
- **Enforcement gaps.** Underfunded regulators, small penalties, cross-border cases and diffuse open-source or small actors make enforcement hard.
- **Overload and uncertainty.** Heavy or changing rules favour incumbents who can afford lawyers and burden small firms and researchers, while repeated postponements leave everyone unsure of what applies.
- **Delay while systems spread.** Protections postponed while systems are deployed leave people exposed in the meantime.
- **Opaque public use.** Governments may use AI in policing, welfare, tax and migration without disclosure, and exempt themselves from rules imposed on others, often in the name of security.
- **Private rule-making.** Companies that control infrastructure and platforms act as de facto regulators through their terms of service.
- **Exclusion.** Affected communities, workers, children and poorer countries may be consulted in form but not in fact.
- **Accountability gaps.** Along a long supply chain each actor can blame another, and victims of opaque systems cannot prove harm.
- **Evidence deficit.** Without incident data and independent testing, regulation is guesswork.
- **Abrupt intervention.** Sudden restrictions with no clear process, reasons or review can be as harmful and unpredictable as the absence of rules.
- **Complexity.** Overlapping regimes confuse both those who must comply and those who are meant to be protected.

## Good practices

- **Build a risk-based legal framework.** Define clear categories, ban the unacceptable, impose assessment, documentation, human oversight, logging and testing on high-risk uses, require transparency, and add specific duties for powerful general-purpose and frontier models, such as evaluations, incident reporting and security. Keep obligations proportionate for small actors and treat open-source development sensibly.
- **Pair law with open standards.** Let the law set the goals and technical standards describe how to meet them. Develop standards in the open, with civil society at the table, and assess conformity through independent bodies.
- **Equip regulators.** Give them expertise, funding, independence and powers to obtain information, inspect systems, order suspension and impose penalties that matter. Add rules against conflicts of interest and revolving doors, and publish their decisions.
- **Create an independent audit ecosystem.** Accredit auditors who are not paid by the firms they audit, offer legal safe harbour to good-faith researchers and red teams, publish audit summaries and support safety bounty programmes.
- **Make liability clear.** Allocate responsibility across developers, deployers and users, apply strict liability to certain high-risk harms, require disclosure of evidence that only the owner holds, mandate insurance for high-risk uses, and provide collective redress, legal aid and compensation funds.
- **Require incident reporting and registers.** Serious incidents are reported to authorities within a set time and recorded in an anonymised public database. High-risk and public-sector systems appear in public registers, and whistleblowers are protected.
- **Lead by example in the public sector.** Assess the impact of public systems before use, list them, make their decisions explainable and contestable, set conditions for procurement and keep security exemptions narrow and overseen.
- **Open real participation.** Use citizens' assemblies, consultations that respond to what is said, worker and union involvement, voices of young people, local hearings, funding for civil society, plain-language materials and enough time to respond.
- **Cooperate internationally.** Support multilateral processes such as the United Nations dialogue and scientific panel, the Council of Europe convention, and the work of the OECD and other forums. Agree on shared red lines and frontier-risk standards, share incident information, build capacity in lower-income countries and be transparent about export controls.
- **Govern adaptively.** Set statutory review dates, use sunset clauses for fast-moving provisions and run regulatory sandboxes that never suspend fundamental rights. Support independent scientific advisory bodies, define triggers for stronger measures, give clear compliance timelines and explain any change.
- **Govern inside companies.** Give boards and executives clear responsibility, give safety and ethics teams real authority, publish safety frameworks with independent verification, protect staff who raise concerns and publish transparency reports.
- **Guard competition.** Make sure that regulation does not entrench the largest firms, and support public-interest alternatives.
- **Fund evidence.** Support independent research, open data on AI use and evaluation of whether the rules actually work.
- **As a citizen, use your voice.** Learn your rights, use complaint channels, take part in consultations, support organisations that defend them and vote.

## Concrete example

*Two hypothetical cases.*

**The launch of a powerful model.** A company is about to release a new general-purpose model.

In the **wrong version**, there are only voluntary pledges. No one outside the company has tested it, and the regulator learns what it can do from the press. The company releases it where the rules are weakest. When the system is used to run a large scam, victims cannot find out who is responsible. The company blames the users, the cloud provider blames the company, and no incident is reported. The public was never consulted.

In the **right version**, the developer must submit evaluations and a safety framework. Independent testers check them, and the developer must report serious incidents. The regulator can request information and suspend deployment. Liability is clear, the system is registered, and consultations covered high-risk uses. Details are shared with international partners. After the scam, victims are compensated through liability and insurance, the lessons are published, and the rules are updated at the next review.

**A city's welfare algorithm.** A city wants to use AI to detect benefit fraud.

In the **wrong version**, the contract is secret, citizens are not told, there is no impact assessment and no one can appeal.

In the **right version**, the system appears in a public register, its impact was assessed and discussed at a public hearing, an independent audit checks it regularly, and every flagged person can have a human review.

## What must not be done

- Writing rules in secret with only industry at the table, or allowing regulated companies to certify their own compliance.
- Granting blanket exemptions for national security, public bodies, research or "innovation" that remove rights protections without independent oversight.
- Passing laws without the means to enforce them, or repeatedly postponing protections for systems already in use without public explanation.
- Treating voluntary pledges as a substitute for binding rules.
- Using sandboxes or pilots to escape rights protections, or to test on people without their consent.
- Passing responsibility along the supply chain until nobody is liable.
- Punishing whistleblowers, researchers and journalists who expose risks, or obstructing good-faith independent testing.
- Concealing incidents, or running systems that authorities cannot inspect.
- Moving to the weakest jurisdiction to avoid rules, or exporting practices that are prohibited at home.
- Excluding affected communities, workers, young people and poorer countries from decisions.
- Using emergency powers or sudden restrictions without a legal basis, stated reasons, a time limit and review.
- Designing governance so heavy that only the largest firms can comply, or so fragmented that nobody can.
- Using "ethics" statements as a substitute for accountability.

## How to remedy

When governance has failed and harm has followed:

1. **Deal with the harm first.** Suspend the system where necessary, notify those affected, apply interim measures and compensate through the courts or regulators.
2. **Investigate the failure of governance itself.** Conduct an independent inquiry into who knew, which rules failed and why enforcement did not act, and publish it.
3. **Close the gaps.** Amend the rules, increase enforcement resources, extend red lines and clarify liability.
4. **Hold people to account.** Sanction companies and officials who broke the rules or concealed information, and remove conflicts of interest.
5. **Open the process.** Publish registers, incident data and audit results, and invite the public and researchers to examine them.
6. **Coordinate.** Share findings with other countries, align rules and strengthen mutual assistance.
7. **Strengthen participation.** Give those who were harmed a formal role in reform, and fund civil society.
8. **Review regularly.** Schedule reviews, measure whether the rules work and adjust them.

**If you believe an AI system affects you unlawfully,** ask the organisation concerned for an explanation, file a complaint with the competent regulator, data-protection authority or equality body, seek collective redress, contact consumer or civil-society organisations and take part in public consultations.

Rules are how a society keeps its promises to itself. Good governance does not hold technology back. It is what allows many people to go further together because they can trust one another.
