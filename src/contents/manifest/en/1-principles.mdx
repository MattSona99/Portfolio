Every technology carries the values of those who build and use it. Artificial intelligence is no exception, with one difference: it acts at a scale and a speed at which errors and abuses can reach millions of people before anyone notices. Before any rule about weapons, work, health or the planet, we need a shared ground. This chapter is that ground.

## Principle

**1.1 The person comes first.** AI exists to serve human beings, never the reverse. Every system must be judged by what it does to people, not only by what it can do.

**1.2 Dignity is not negotiable.** No efficiency, profit, security or convenience justifies treating a person as a data point, a cost or an obstacle.

**1.3 Rights apply in full.** Human rights do not pause when a decision is made by software. Existing rights, such as those in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and in the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, apply to AI exactly as they apply to everything else.

**1.4 Responsibility stays human.** Whatever one believes about the nature of future systems, accountability for what an AI does must always be traceable to people and institutions. "The algorithm decided" is never an answer.

**1.5 Benefit must be shared.** The value of an AI system is measured by who it helps and who it harms. The burden of proof lies with those who deploy it, not with those who are affected by it.

**1.6 Humility before power.** We are building systems we do not fully understand. The greater and less reversible the possible harm, the greater the caution required.

## Risks

- **Erosion of dignity.** Opaque automated decisions about credit, jobs, welfare or justice can reduce people to scores and profiles.
- **Diffusion of responsibility.** When many actors contribute to a system, each can claim that someone else is accountable.
- **Concentration of power.** The ability to build and run advanced AI is concentrated in few hands, and so is the power to decide how it is used.
- **Gradual normalisation.** Small abuses, accepted one at a time, become the new standard.
- **Convenience over rights.** The most useful system is often the most intrusive, and the trade-off is rarely visible to those who pay for it.

## Good practices

- **Write the purpose down.** Before building or adopting an AI system, state what it is for, who benefits and who could be harmed.
- **Assess the human impact.** Run a documented impact assessment, in the same spirit as a data protection impact assessment, before deployment and after every major change.
- **Name an accountable owner.** Every system must have a named person or team who answers for it.
- **Guarantee explanation and appeal.** People affected by a decision have the right to understand it, to contest it and to reach a human being.
- **Choose the least intrusive option.** If a simpler, less invasive tool does the job, use it.
- **Involve those affected.** Workers, patients, citizens and communities should be consulted before a system that concerns them goes live.
- **Keep records.** Log decisions, versions and known limitations so that audits and investigations are possible.

## Concrete example

*A hypothetical case.* A city adopts an AI system to rank applications for housing support.

In the **wrong version**, the system produces a score, caseworkers follow it by default, and applicants receive a rejection with no reason and no way to appeal. Nobody can say who is responsible for errors.

In the **right version**, the system only helps to sort the queue. A caseworker makes every decision and records the reasons. Applicants are told that an AI tool was used, can ask for a human review within a fixed time, and the city publishes a yearly audit showing whether outcomes differ across groups of applicants.

## What must not be done

- Using AI to deny fundamental rights or essential services without meaningful human review.
- Scoring or ranking citizens on their general social behaviour.
- Exploiting the vulnerability of children, patients, the elderly or the poor.
- Hiding behind the system: blaming "the algorithm" for decisions that people chose to automate.
- Deploying first and asking questions later when the possible harm is serious or irreversible.
- Making people believe they are dealing with a human, or with an authority, when they are not.

## How to remedy

When a system is found to violate these principles:

1. **Suspend it** or restore human decision-making immediately, until the problem is understood.
2. **Inform those affected**, clearly and without delay, and offer them a way to have their case reviewed.
3. **Repair the harm**: correct wrong decisions, and compensate where damage has been done.
4. **Find the root cause** and publish what was learned, so that others can avoid the same error.
5. **Submit it to independent audit** before any return to service.
6. **Update the rules.** Every failure is a reason to improve this manifesto and the practices that follow from it.

Everything that follows in this manifesto grows from one idea: technology is judged by the human lives it respects, protects and improves.
