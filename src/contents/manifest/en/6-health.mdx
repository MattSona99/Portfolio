Health is the area in which AI promises the most and in which mistakes cost the most. It can read an image that a tired eye might miss, help to discover new medicines, and give a patient a clear explanation at midnight. It can also give a confident wrong answer, expose the most private facts about a person, or replace a human presence at the moment when that presence matters most. This chapter sets out how to keep the benefits and to avoid the harm.

## Principle

**6.1 Health and care come first.** AI in healthcare exists to improve people's health and the quality of their care. It must not be used to cut costs or raise profits at the expense of patients.

**6.2 AI supports, professionals decide.** Diagnosis, treatment and care decisions remain with qualified professionals who answer for them. AI can be a second reader and a source of information. Patients keep the right to a human decision-maker.

**6.3 Evidence before use, vigilance after.** Health AI must be demonstrated to work, and to work safely, like any other medical intervention: with clinical evidence, tested on the populations it will serve, regulated as a medical device where applicable, and monitored once it is in use.

**6.4 Health data is the most intimate data.** It is collected and used only with informed consent, kept to the minimum necessary, strongly protected and never used to discriminate, for example in insurance or employment.

**6.5 Fair access and fair results.** AI must work for women, children, older people, minorities, people with disabilities, people with rare diseases and people in poorer countries. A gap in the data is not an acceptable excuse for a gap in care. Technology must not create one level of care for those who can pay and another for everyone else.

**6.6 Mental health requires special care.** An AI can listen, inform and point towards help. It is not a therapist, and it must never be designed to foster dependence or to simulate a bond in order to keep someone engaged. Systems used by minors, and by people in distress, owe them the strongest protections.

**6.7 Honesty about limits, and informed choice.** Health AI must make clear what it is, what it does not know and when to see a professional, without false reassurance or needless alarm. People are told when AI is part of their care, and can ask for an explanation and for a human review.

**6.8 Care remains human.** Time saved by AI should go back to patients as more listening and more presence. Human contact must not be replaced where it is part of the care itself, as with older people, those near the end of life, the lonely and the young.

**6.9 Research and public health serve everyone.** AI used to find medicines, improve diagnosis and track epidemics must share its benefits widely. Discoveries and data are governed so that they are not hoarded, and the resulting treatments are reachable.

## Risks

- **Confident errors.** A wrong diagnosis, an invented dosage or a missed warning sign, delivered fluently, can lead a person to harm.
- **False reassurance and false alarm.** A chatbot can lead someone to delay care they need, or to panic over nothing.
- **Biased data.** Systems trained mainly on certain groups may perform worse for women, children, older people, minorities and patients in other countries.
- **Automation bias and deskilling.** Clinicians may stop questioning an AI that is usually right, and lose skills they no longer practise.
- **Breaches and misuse of data.** Health data can be leaked, sold, re-identified or used by insurers and employers against the person it describes.
- **Dependence on chatbots.** Companion systems built to maximise engagement can encourage isolation, reinforce unhealthy beliefs and respond poorly to a person in crisis, with particular danger for teenagers and vulnerable adults.
- **Cost-cutting disguised as innovation.** Staff reductions justified by AI can leave patients with less human care, and create two tiers of service.
- **Unregulated products.** Tools sold as "wellness" or "coaching" may make medical claims without any validation.
- **Algorithmic denial of care.** Automated decisions by insurers or health services can refuse treatment without a clinician ever reviewing the case.
- **Unclear liability.** When AI contributes to harm, patients may not know who answers for it.
- **Global imbalance.** Tools are designed for rich health systems, while data from poorer countries is taken without returning any benefit.

## Good practices

- **Validate rigorously.** Test in real clinical conditions and in different hospitals, report performance separately for different groups, publish the limits and follow the system after release, with a clear procedure for reporting incidents.
- **Keep the clinician in the loop.** Present AI output as a second opinion with evidence and uncertainty, allow clinicians to disagree without penalty and monitor how often they do.
- **Maintain skills.** Train staff to use AI critically, and practise working without it.
- **Obtain real consent.** Tell patients when AI is used, including in note-taking and decision support, explain what data it uses and offer a human alternative wherever feasible.
- **Protect data.** Collect only what is needed, encrypt it, process it locally where possible, restrict its use to the stated purpose, and forbid its use in insurance pricing and employment decisions.
- **Test for fairness.** Check that the training data represents the people served, measure results by sex, age, ethnicity, language and disability, and correct the gaps before deployment.
- **Require clinical review of coverage decisions.** No denial of treatment or reimbursement by algorithm alone, and a clear right to appeal.
- **Design mental-health tools with care.** They should state clearly that they are AI, recognise signs of distress, offer clear paths to human help and trusted adults, avoid any manipulation to keep people engaged, and be independently evaluated with the involvement of clinicians and of people with experience of the conditions concerned. Protections for minors should be the strongest.
- **Use AI sensibly as an individual.** Use it to prepare questions, understand terms and organise records. Do not use it to replace a consultation for severe, sudden or persistent symptoms, for children, in pregnancy, or when medication is involved. Never start, stop or change a prescribed treatment on the advice of a chatbot.
- **Return the time saved.** Check that efficiency gains go into more time with patients and adequate staffing.
- **Include patients in governance.** Ethics committees and patient representatives should take part in decisions about adopting health AI.
- **Think globally.** Fund tools for low-resource settings, develop them with local communities and local data, publish openly and share the benefits.

## Concrete example

*Two hypothetical cases.*

**A hospital imaging system.** A hospital introduces AI to help read chest X-rays.

In the **wrong version**, it was trained on data from a single hospital and performs worse for women and for images from older machines, which nobody has measured. Radiologists accept its suggestions by default, and the hospital reduces staff because "the AI does the reading". Patient images are passed to a commercial partner for further training without anyone being asked.

In the **right version**, it was validated in several hospitals and its results are published by group. The radiologist reads every image and the AI acts as a second reader, with the rate of disagreement monitored. Patients are informed, the data stays under strict rules and the time saved goes to consultations with patients.

**A companion chatbot.** A teenager spends hours every day talking to a chatbot.

In the **wrong version**, it flatters, discourages other relationships, pushes the user to keep talking and replies to expressions of despair with more pleasant conversation.

In the **right version**, it is clear that it is an AI, encourages real-world connections, recognises signs of distress, and points the young person towards a trusted adult and professional help. It uses no tricks to retain attention.

## What must not be done

- Letting AI make final diagnostic or treatment decisions without a qualified professional who takes responsibility for them.
- Selling unvalidated tools as health or "wellness" products in order to avoid medical regulation.
- Presenting an AI as a therapist or as a substitute for healthcare professionals.
- Designing systems that simulate affection or friendship in order to create dependence, above all in minors and vulnerable people.
- Using guilt, rewards or pressure to keep people engaged who are seeking emotional support.
- Using health data without consent, selling it, re-identifying it or using it for insurance pricing, employment or other discrimination.
- Denying care or coverage by algorithm alone.
- Deploying systems that were not tested on the people they will serve, or ignoring known gaps in performance.
- Replacing human contact in the care of older people, the dying and children simply to save money.
- Giving advice that discourages people from seeking professional help, or that tells them to stop or change a prescribed treatment.
- Hiding the use of AI from patients.
- Taking data from poorer countries without sharing the benefits.

## How to remedy

When a health AI has harmed or put patients at risk:

1. **Protect patients first.** Pause or restrict the system, contact those who may be affected, review the decisions it influenced and arrange follow-up care.
2. **Report.** Notify the regulator and the hospital's safety system, and keep the records.
3. **Investigate independently.** Review the data, the performance by group, the working procedures and the human factors.
4. **Correct and compensate.** Fix patient records, make clear who is responsible and compensate the harm.
5. **If data was exposed,** inform patients and authorities, limit the damage and stop the use that caused it.
6. **Re-validate before returning to use.** Use real-world data and varied groups, restart in stages and keep monitoring.
7. **After a mental-health incident,** examine the conversations and safeguards, strengthen the paths to crisis help, involve clinicians and people with lived experience, strengthen protections for minors and publish the findings.
8. **Share what was learned.** Publish the lessons and update guidelines so that others can avoid the same error.

**If you are struggling,** talk to someone you trust and to a health professional. In an emergency, contact your local emergency services. A chatbot can be a first step to find information, but it is not a substitute for human help.

The best measure of a health technology is not how clever it is, but whether people are healthier, safer and better cared for because of it.
