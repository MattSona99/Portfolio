AI is often described as the product of a few laboratories. In truth it is made from the writing, images and knowledge of billions of people, the work of data labellers and engineers on every continent, minerals dug from many countries, public research and shared infrastructure. The question of this chapter is who gets what in return: who holds the power, who pays the price and who is left outside.

## Principle

**13.1 AI is a shared inheritance.** It is built on humanity's collective knowledge and on public investment. Its benefits must return to people broadly, through fair access, fair taxation of the wealth it creates and support for those it displaces. This is the eighth pillar of this manifesto: no person, population or worker is sacrificed to profit.

**13.2 No digital colonialism.** Countries and communities must not be reduced to suppliers of raw materials, data and cheap labour for systems that they neither control nor benefit from.

**13.3 Access is a foundation, not a luxury.** Connectivity, devices, skills and affordable AI tools are part of development. The price of access must not be out of reach of those who need it most, and quality must not depend on the wealth of the user.

**13.4 Build capacity, not dependency.** Every region should be able to develop, adapt, evaluate and govern AI for itself, with access to computing power, data, talent, language resources and institutions.

**13.5 Power must stay accountable.** No small group of companies or states may control the chips, the cloud, the data and the models on which everyone else depends, and decide for the world. Concentrated power requires competition, openness and oversight.

**13.6 The supply chain is part of the product.** Everyone who contributes, from miners and factory workers to data labellers, moderators and drivers, deserves fair pay, safety and rights. Companies answer for the conditions of their suppliers.

**13.7 Communities own their data, knowledge and languages.** Local and indigenous communities have the right to decide how their data, culture and languages are used, and to share in the benefits. Taking without consent and without return is not acceptable.

**13.8 Finite resources are shared fairly.** The energy, water, land and minerals consumed by AI are weighed against basic human needs everywhere. The demand of richer places must not crowd out the essential needs of poorer ones.

**13.9 Everyone has a seat where the rules are made.** All countries and communities take part in setting the standards that will govern AI, with the means to participate effectively and to enforce what is agreed.

## Risks

- **The compute divide.** Most advanced computing capacity sits in a few countries, so everyone else rents access that can be priced, restricted or withdrawn.
- **The usage gap.** Adoption is lower in poorer countries, where subscriptions can cost a large share of income and connectivity and devices are scarce.
- **Dependency and loss of sovereignty.** Countries that rely on foreign models, clouds and data pipelines may gain access while losing practical control over standards, safeguards and local fit. Public services can become hostage to a supplier's terms.
- **Concentrated corporate power.** When one group controls chips, cloud, models and applications together, it can lock in customers, shut out rivals, shape regulation and capture much of the value.
- **Extractive supply chains.** Mining, hardware assembly and data work often depend on low pay, insecure contracts, trauma and the suppression of organising, hidden behind layers of subcontractors.
- **Extraction of data and culture.** Languages, art, local knowledge and the health data of poorer populations can be taken to build products that those same people cannot afford or that do not serve them.
- **Competition for essentials.** Data centres can compete with households and farms for electricity and water, and local prices can rise.
- **Fiscal erosion.** Tax holidays, profit shifting and a race to attract investment can leave host countries with little revenue and heavy infrastructure costs.
- **Brain drain.** Talent flows towards a few hubs, leaving other regions without the people needed to build their own capacity.
- **Geopolitical fragmentation.** Treating AI purely as a strategic weapon pushes countries into blocs, squeezes cooperation and leaves smaller states with no good choice.
- **Absence from governance.** Many countries are not at the table where global rules are written.
- **Gains captured by capital.** Within countries as well as between them, the rewards of automation may flow mainly to the owners of the systems.

## Good practices

- **Invest in public goods.** Fund open models, open datasets and shared scientific computing, support pooled regional compute and give researchers in every region access to resources.
- **Make access affordable.** Offer pricing that reflects income, free or low-cost basic access for education, health and public services, and tools that work offline, on low bandwidth or on modest devices. Set up community access points in schools and libraries.
- **Build local capacity.** Support training, scholarships and local research labs, fellowships that encourage return, regional centres of excellence and genuine technology transfer on equal terms. Help local start-ups and public bodies adopt AI, and let communities build their own language resources.
- **Keep choices open.** Require open standards, interoperability, data portability and exit clauses in public contracts, allow multiple suppliers and local hosting options, and keep critical public services free of lock-in.
- **Keep power in check.** Apply competition law to the whole chain from chips to applications, review mergers and exclusive deals, protect interoperability, support public-interest alternatives and publish ownership and lobbying.
- **Hold supply chains to account.** Map them, publish supplier lists, audit them and apply binding due-diligence rules on human rights and the environment. Ensure living wages, clear contracts, the freedom to organise, grievance channels without retaliation, and prices that make decent pay possible for suppliers.
- **Respect data sovereignty.** Obtain the free, prior and informed consent of communities, use recognised frameworks for indigenous and community data governance, agree on benefit-sharing, credit sources and return results and tools in local languages.
- **Put essential needs first.** Site facilities with priority for local water and electricity, require developers to add capacity and not divert it, share infrastructure such as grids, water systems and connectivity, and make contracts public.
- **Be fair in taxation.** Tax profits where value is created, coordinate internationally to prevent a race to the bottom, grant incentives only in exchange for real returns and use the revenue for public services and for people affected by the transition. Consider funds that distribute a share of AI gains to the public.
- **Open the table.** Fund and support the participation of poorer countries and of civil society, workers and communities in standard-setting, strengthen regional and South-South cooperation and ensure that scientific assessments reflect every region.
- **Start from real needs.** Prioritise uses that communities themselves identify, such as farming advice, health diagnostics, early warning of floods and droughts and education in local languages, and design, run and maintain them with local people.
- **Be transparent.** Publish where companies operate, what they pay in wages, taxes and royalties, and what resources they use.
- **Choose well as a customer.** Prefer suppliers who publish information on their supply chains and treat workers fairly, and support organisations of data workers.

## Concrete example

*Two hypothetical cases.*

**A country adopts AI for public services.** A government in a lower-income country wants AI for health and agriculture, and turns to a foreign provider.

In the **wrong version**, the contract is closed and its terms are secret. Citizens' data is sent abroad, the models work poorly in local languages, prices in foreign currency rise with use and there is no way out. The labelling of local data is done by underpaid contractors. A new data centre, granted a long tax holiday, draws on scarce water and power during shortages, and mineral revenues stay out of public view. The country has no voice when standards are set, and one day the supplier changes its terms.

In the **right version**, the contract is open and public-interest. Data stays under national or regional control, and models are adapted to local languages together with local researchers. Open standards and exit clauses protect against lock-in, regional compute is shared and training and technology transfer are part of the agreement. Local data workers have fair contracts and a voice. The data centre gives priority to local water and power and pays its fair share, mining and tax revenues are transparent and invested publicly, and the country takes part in setting the rules.

**A community's language archive.** An indigenous community has recorded its language and stories over generations.

In the **wrong version**, the recordings are scraped to train a translation system that is then sold back to the community as a paid service.

In the **right version**, the community decides whether and how the material is used, shares in the ownership and in any revenue, has free access to the tools and keeps the right to withdraw.

## What must not be done

- Treating poorer countries and communities as sources of raw materials, data and cheap labour while excluding them from decisions and benefits.
- Extracting data, languages, cultural heritage or indigenous knowledge without consent and fair return.
- Building dependency by design, with lock-in, opaque terms, no exit and no data portability, in public services.
- Abusing market power by tying chips, cloud, models and applications together to exclude competitors, or by capturing regulators through lobbying.
- Moving harmful work offshore to avoid labour protection, hiding behind subcontractors, paying below a living wage or retaliating against workers who organise.
- Siting facilities that take essential water or electricity from local people, or keeping the terms of such contracts secret.
- Shifting profits to avoid tax where value is created, or demanding blanket tax holidays without a real return.
- Cutting off essential public services that depend on AI without notice and a way to transition.
- Excluding countries, communities and workers from the making of standards.
- Using financing terms that trap countries in debt for infrastructure they cannot control.

## How to remedy

When AI practices have deepened inequality or exploited people and places:

1. **Pause and reopen.** Suspend contracts, permits or projects that harm local communities, and renegotiate with those communities at the table.
2. **Restore and compensate.** Pay what is owed, compensate communities and workers, return data and restore water, power and land that have been affected.
3. **Open the books.** Publish contracts, ownership, supply chains, taxes and royalties, and submit them to independent audit.
4. **Enforce.** Let competition, labour and human-rights authorities investigate, apply supply-chain liability, cooperate across borders and make courts accessible to affected workers and communities, including in the countries where the companies are based.
5. **Build capacity.** Fund local computing, talent and institutions, transfer technology and support open alternatives.
6. **Share the gains.** Apply fair taxation and royalties, and set up benefit-sharing funds.
7. **Give a seat.** Involve affected states, communities and workers in decisions, and support their participation.
8. **Keep measuring.** Track access, prices, wages, concentration and who benefits, and review the rules regularly.

**As a user or an organisation,** ask where your services come from, prefer providers that treat workers fairly and publish their supply chains, and avoid uses of AI that depend on exploiting others.

A technology built from everyone's knowledge cannot belong fairly to only a few. The test of this chapter is simple: after AI, are the least powerful better able to shape their own future, or less?
